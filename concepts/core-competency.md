# Core Competency

Introduced by [C.K. Prahalad](../people/ck-prahalad.md) and Gary Hamel in their landmark 1990 HBR article "The Core Competence of the Corporation." The concept that redefined how companies think about competitive advantage — shifting the lens from **products and business units** to the **underlying capabilities** that produce them.

> "A company is not a portfolio of businesses — it is a portfolio of competencies."

## Definition

A core competency is a **bundle of skills and technologies** that:

1. **Provides access to a wide variety of markets** — it can be applied across multiple products and services
2. **Makes a significant contribution to perceived customer benefits** — customers care about what it enables, even if they can't name it
3. **Is difficult for competitors to imitate** — it's embedded in organisational learning and culture, not easily replicated

The classic example: Honda's core competency is engine technology. That competency powers motorcycles, cars, lawnmowers, generators, outboard motors, and jet engines. Honda doesn't have one business — it has one deep competency expressed across many markets.

## The Tree Analogy

Prahalad and Hamel used the analogy of a tree:
- **Roots** = core competencies (the invisible foundation)
- **Trunk** = core products (the manufactured expression of competencies)
- **Major branches** = business units
- **Leaves, flowers, fruit** = end products

Most competitive analysis focuses on leaves (products). Prahalad argued you must understand the roots. A company can lose leaves (products fail) and survive if the roots (competencies) are strong. But if you attack the roots — erode the core competencies — the whole tree dies.

## Core Products vs End Products

A critical distinction:

- **Core product** — the physical embodiment of a core competency; sold to other businesses, often invisibly to end consumers (e.g. Honda small engines, Canon laser printer engines)
- **End product** — what the final customer buys (Honda Civic, Canon photocopier)

Companies that understand core products can be the hidden leaders in an industry — building capability that many end-product manufacturers depend on.

## The Strategic Implication: Competency vs Business Unit

The traditional view: a diversified company is a portfolio of **business units**. Each unit is evaluated on its own P&L. Corporate allocates capital to the best-performing units and sells the underperformers.

The Prahalad/Hamel view: this destroys value. When you evaluate by business unit, you:
- Fragment the organisation into silos that don't share capabilities
- Sell "underperforming" units that actually hold critical competencies
- Miss the possibility of **bundling** competencies across units to create new markets

The better view: a diversified company is a portfolio of **core competencies**. The question is not "which business should we be in?" but "which competencies do we need to build, and which markets can they open?"

## Identifying Core Competencies

Questions to ask:
- What are we uniquely good at that transcends individual products?
- What capabilities would cripple us if we lost them?
- What skills do our competitors struggle to replicate even after years of trying?
- What customer benefits do we enable that others cannot?

**Warning signs you've confused a core competency:**
- A function that most competitors also perform (payroll processing is not a core competency)
- A single technology that could be bought from outside
- Market position or brand (these are outcomes of core competencies, not the thing itself)

## Connection to [Specific Knowledge](./specific-knowledge.md) — Naval Ravikant

Prahalad's core competency at the corporate level maps almost exactly to [Naval](../people/naval-ravikant.md)'s [specific knowledge](./specific-knowledge.md) at the individual level:

| Prahalad's Core Competency | Naval's Specific Knowledge |
|---|---|
| Hard to imitate | Cannot be trained for |
| Built through continuous learning | Built through genuine curiosity |
| Enables access to multiple markets | Enables leverage across multiple opportunities |
| Embedded in the organisation | Embedded in the person |
| Produces sustainable competitive advantage | Produces irreplaceable value |

Both argue: durable advantage comes from deep, embedded capability — not surface features, not products, not market position.

## Connection to [Amazon Flywheel](./amazon-growth-flywheel.md)

Amazon's core competency is arguably **logistics and fulfilment technology + data infrastructure**. AWS is the core product expression of the data infrastructure competency — sold to other businesses. This is exactly the Prahalad/Hamel structure: one deep competency, multiple end-product markets.
