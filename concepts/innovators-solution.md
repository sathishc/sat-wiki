# The Innovator's Solution

[Clayton Christensen's](../people/clayton-christensen.md) second major work (2003, co-authored with Michael Raynor). Where [*The Innovator's Dilemma*](./innovators-dilemma.md) diagnoses *why* great companies fail, *The Innovator's Solution* prescribes *what to do* — both to attack incumbents and to defend against disruption.

## The Core Move: From Diagnosis to Prescription

The Dilemma left a question unanswered: if the failure is structural and systemic, what can a manager actually *do*? The Solution answers it with a set of concrete frameworks.

---

## The RPM Framework

The most important strategic lens in the book. Every organisation has three layers:

| Layer | Definition | Character |
|---|---|---|
| **Resources** | What the company *has* — capital, people, technology, brand, data | Most visible; easiest to acquire/discard |
| **Processes** | How the company *does things* — workflows, decision rules, culture, ways of working | Less visible; slower to change; not transferable |
| **Values** | What the company *prioritises* — margin thresholds, customer definitions, what counts as "good enough" | Most invisible; most durable; most dangerous |

### Why This Matters for Disruption

Acquisitions and internal innovation projects routinely fail because leaders focus on **resources** (we hired the right people, we bought the right company) while ignoring **processes** and **values**.

A large incumbent acquiring a disruptive startup gets the startup's resources. But then it applies its own processes (approval chains, margin requirements, customer review cycles) and its own values (must hit X% gross margin, must serve enterprise customers). The disruptive potential is killed — not through bad intent, but through RPM misalignment.

**Corollary:** Capabilities live in processes and values, not in resources. The most capable people, operating inside the wrong processes and values, will produce the wrong outcomes.

### The Implication for Disruption Defence

To successfully pursue a disruptive opportunity, you need **separate RPM**:
- Separate P&L with its own margin expectations
- Separate processes (faster, more exploratory, lower rigour on some dimensions)
- Separate values (it's *okay* to serve low-margin customers; winning market share matters more than current profit)

The alternative — embedding the disruptive unit inside the parent — usually fails. The parent's values will allocate resources away from the unit whenever there's competition for capital.

## How to Pick Disruptive Battles

### Target Non-Consumption

The highest-probability target for a new disruptive entrant is **non-consumption** — people who are not using existing solutions because they are:
- Too expensive
- Too complicated
- Require too much skill to operate
- Not accessible in the right context

Non-consumers have nothing to compare against, so "good enough" is genuinely good. Incumbents have no incentive to serve them (small market, thin margins). And serving them doesn't require competing head-on.

### Or Target the Low End

The second option: target overserved customers at the low end of an existing market. These customers are paying for more than they need. A simpler, cheaper product gets hired.

### Don't Aim Up-Market First

The fatal error of many would-be disruptors: aiming at the incumbent's best customers from day one. This forces you to compete on the incumbent's terms (performance, features, relationships) at a time when you are inferior. Start below; improve; let the up-market trajectory happen naturally.

## Integration vs. Modularisation

A key framework for understanding *where profit lives* in any [Value Chain](./value-chain.md) at any point in time.

**Rule:**
- **Integrate** (control more of the value chain in-house) when performance is **not yet good enough** — the product doesn't yet do what customers need, and interdependencies between components determine performance
- **Modularise / outsource** when performance **exceeds what customers need** — the product overshoots, components become interchangeable, and standardisation occurs

**Why this matters:**
Profit migrates to the layer in the value chain that is *not yet good enough*. Once a layer overshoots, it commoditises and margins collapse. A company must stay on the right side of this line.

**Example trajectory:**
- IBM in the 1980s: integrated (hardware + OS + software) because the whole system was not yet good enough. Profit captured across the stack.
- IBM licensed the OS to Microsoft and the chip to Intel because it assumed those were commodities. But they were actually the undershooting layers — and all profit migrated there.
- Microsoft and Intel held the "not yet good enough" layers; IBM was left with the assembly of commodities.

## Hiring for Experiences

When staffing a disruptive initiative or a new venture, credentials (degrees, past titles) are poor predictors. What matters is **the experiences the person has accumulated** — specifically, whether they have navigated the *same type of challenge* the new role requires.

This is a JTBD lens applied to hiring: what job does this role need done? Who has been through experiences that prepared them for exactly that job?

Managers who have only operated in established, high-resource environments are often the wrong hire for a resource-constrained, ambiguous startup context — regardless of their credentials.

## Emergent vs. Deliberate Strategy

For new ventures and early-stage companies, strategy should be **emergent**, not planned.

- Deliberate strategy: execute a pre-formed plan
- Emergent strategy: test hypotheses, observe what works, update accordingly

The danger of deliberate strategy in novel contexts: you lock in assumptions before you have data. Christensen (echoing Mintzberg) argues that companies should treat early-stage strategy as a portfolio of hypotheses — and allocate resources to whichever hypotheses are proving out.

This connects to [Dalio's](../people/ray-dalio.md) principle of updating beliefs based on evidence, and to [Kahneman's](../people/daniel-kahneman.md) outside view: avoid committing too early to an inside-view narrative.

---

## Practical Prescriptions

1. **Create a separate organisation** for disruptive businesses — dedicated P&L, processes, values
2. **Start with non-consumption** — compete against no consumption, not against incumbents
3. **Hire for experiences**, not credentials
4. **Identify the integration/modularisation frontier** — and be on the right side of it
5. **Keep strategy emergent early** — test, observe, adapt before committing to a deliberate plan
6. **Don't rescue bad money with good** — know when to cut a failing disruptive effort vs. when to persist
