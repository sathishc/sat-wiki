# Authority Bias

The fourth of [Cialdini's](../people/robert-cialdini.md) [seven principles of influence](./principles-of-influence.md). We tend to comply with people who have legitimate authority or expertise — and with the *symbols* of authority, even absent genuine expertise.

## The Mechanism

Deference to authority is rational on average: experts usually do know more than we do. The heuristic fails when:
- **Symbols substitute for genuine expertise** (titles, uniforms, expensive cars)
- **Domain expertise is over-extended** (a brilliant physicist opining on nutrition)
- **Authority is staged or fabricated** (fake credentials, misleading titles)
- The authority figure has **no skin in the game** — bears no consequences for being wrong

## Symbols vs. Substance

Cialdini's key finding: we respond to *symbols* of authority almost as strongly as to genuine authority. People in uniforms, with impressive titles, or driving expensive cars trigger deference automatically — the [halo effect](./cognitive-biases-kahneman.md) transfers perceived authority across domains.

This is why:
- White lab coats increase compliance with medical instructions
- "Dr." before a name increases persuasion even in non-medical contexts
- Expensive suits and watches signal competence regardless of actual competence

## Classic Evidence

**Milgram experiments (1963):** Ordinary people administered what they believed were dangerous electric shocks to strangers — escalating to potentially lethal levels — because a person in a lab coat told them to. ~65% went to maximum voltage. The authority figure's physical presence, uniform, and institutional affiliation were sufficient.

**Nurses and doctors:** Research found nurses frequently complied with clearly incorrect medication orders from "doctors" (including fake instructions from unknown callers) due to the authority of the title.

## The Credibility Signal: Acknowledging Weaknesses

Counterintuitively, genuine experts often increase compliance by **acknowledging weaknesses** before making their case:
- "This approach has some downsides, but here's why I recommend it..."
- Signals honesty and completeness — "they're not hiding anything"
- Increases trust more than unqualified praise would

This distinguishes genuine authority from manufactured authority, which rarely acknowledges limitations.

## Cross-references

**↔ [Skin in the Game](./skin-in-the-game.md) (Taleb)**
Skin in the Game is the structural filter for authority. An authority figure who bears the consequences of their advice has aligned incentives — their authority signal is informative. An authority who advises without consequences can be wrong indefinitely without personal cost. Taleb's rule: don't listen to those who don't have skin in the game. This is the most robust defence against authority bias.

**↔ [Idea Meritocracy](./idea-meritocracy.md) (Dalio)**
Dalio's explicit weighting of opinions by **track record** rather than title is an institutional antidote to authority bias. Bridgewater's "believability-weighted" system asks: has this person been right about things like this before? Title is irrelevant; demonstrated competence is the metric.

**↔ [Cognitive Biases — Halo Effect](./cognitive-biases-kahneman.md)**
The halo effect is the cognitive mechanism behind authority symbol power: positive impressions in one dimension (a lab coat, a luxury car) transfer to perceived competence in unrelated dimensions. Authority bias and the halo effect are the same mechanism at different scales.

## Defence

Ask:
1. Is this person actually an expert in *this specific domain*?
2. Are the credentials genuine and relevant?
3. Does their authority come with accountability — do they bear consequences if wrong?
4. Would I be as persuaded if they said the same thing in different clothes?
