# Tail Events and Investing

> "A small number of events account for the majority of outcomes." — Morgan Housel

## One-Line Definition

In investing (and in life), the distribution of outcomes is not a bell curve — a tiny number of extreme events (the tails) drive almost all the results.

## The Argument

Most things in investing fail. Most stock picks underperform. Most venture investments return nothing. Most business initiatives don't work.

And yet — portfolios grow, funds return multiples, businesses succeed. How?

**The winners don't just offset the losers. They dwarf them.** A single Amazon in a portfolio of 50 stocks returns more than all 49 others combined. A single correct macro call can make a decade of returns.

This is the power law applied to investing — the same structure Thiel describes for [startups](../concepts/power-law-startups.md) and Taleb describes as [Extremistan](../people/nassim-taleb.md).

## Practical Implications

### For individual investors:
- **Expect most picks to be wrong.** This is not failure; it's the nature of the distribution.
- **Stay in the game long enough to catch the tails.** Missing the 10 best days of the S&P 500 over 30 years eliminates most of the return. The tail days are unpredictable.
- **Don't let small losses trigger behaviour that causes you to miss the tails.** The biggest investor error is selling during drawdowns and missing the recovery.

### For business:
- Most products fail. Most marketing doesn't work. Most hires are average.
- A few products, a few campaigns, a few hires produce almost all the value.
- This means: high tolerance for small failures + zero tolerance for decisions that prevent you from catching the big wins.

### For decision-making:
- Optimise for *staying in the game*, not for avoiding losses.
- [Room for error](../concepts/room-for-error.md) is the mechanism that keeps you in the game long enough to catch the tail.

## The Emotional Challenge

> "The ability to do nothing is one of the most valuable things in investing."

Tail events require patience that feels irrational. You hold through years of underperformance or sideways movement. You resist the urge to "do something." The psychological cost is real — and it's why most people don't capture the returns available to them, even when they know the theory.
