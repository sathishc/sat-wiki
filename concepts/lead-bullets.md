# Lead Bullets

> "There is no silver bullet for this problem. It's going to require a lot of lead bullets." — Ben Horowitz (quoting a board member)

## One-Line Definition

When a company is in deep trouble, there is no clever shortcut that fixes everything. You have to do the hard work — one bullet at a time.

## The Origin

Horowitz tells the story of Opsware under threat from a better-funded competitor. He was desperately searching for a strategic masterstroke — a pivot, an acquisition, a partnership — that would change the game. A board member told him plainly: *"There is no silver bullet. It's going to require a lot of lead bullets."*

The message: stop looking for the clever move. Start executing the difficult, unglamorous work of actually improving your product and outcompeting.

## Why Founders Keep Looking for Silver Bullets

When a company faces an existential threat, silver-bullet thinking is emotionally understandable:
- The problem feels too big for incremental solutions
- A pivot or acquisition *could* theoretically change the equation
- Looking for a silver bullet feels like strategic thinking (it isn't)
- It allows you to avoid the psychological difficulty of the Struggle

## The Lead Bullet Discipline

Committing to lead bullets means:
1. Accurately diagnosing what's broken
2. Building the roadmap to fix it (even if it takes 18 months)
3. Executing without the hope of a shortcut
4. Trusting the team to close the gap

This is unglamorous. It's also usually correct.

## When Silver Bullets Are Real

Occasionally, a genuine strategic move *does* change the game — an acquisition, a platform shift, a pricing change. Horowitz isn't saying these never exist. He's saying:

> **Don't assume one exists. Default to lead bullets and be genuinely surprised when a silver bullet appears.**
