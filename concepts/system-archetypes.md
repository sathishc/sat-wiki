# System Archetypes

Recurring system structures that produce recognisable, predictable behaviour patterns. Identified by [Peter Senge](../people/peter-senge.md) and [Donella Meadows](../people/donella-meadows.md). Archetypes are the "common stories" of [systems thinking](./systems-thinking.md) — once you can name them, you start seeing them everywhere.

> "Today's problems come from yesterday's solutions." — Senge

---

## The Core Archetypes

### 1. Limits to Growth

**Structure:** A reinforcing loop drives growth → growth strengthens a constraint (balancing loop) → the constraint slows growth → if the constraint is ignored and the accelerator is pushed harder, collapse follows faster.

**Behaviour:** Rapid growth, slowing growth, plateau, possible collapse.

**The trap:** Management sees slowing growth and pushes the accelerator (more sales, more marketing, more resources) instead of addressing the constraint. This accelerates the collision with the limit.

**The fix:** Identify and address the constraint, not the growth loop.

**Examples:**
- Startup growth slows as customer support degrades — hire more salespeople (wrong) or fix support capacity (right)
- A road is congested → build more roads → induced demand fills them → more congestion
- [Crossing the Chasm](./crossing-the-chasm.md): early market growth hits the chasm (the balancing constraint between visionary early adopters and pragmatist mainstream buyers)

---

### 2. Shifting the Burden

**Structure:** A problem produces a symptom. A symptomatic solution (quick fix) relieves the symptom without addressing the underlying problem. Reliance on the quick fix grows. Capacity to implement the fundamental solution atrophies.

**Behaviour:** Short-term relief → growing dependence on the fix → underlying problem worsens → more pressure on the fix.

**The trap:** The quick fix feels like progress. The fundamental solution requires more effort and has delayed results. Over time, the organisation loses the capability to address root causes.

**Examples:**
- **Workarounds in software:** patching bugs in production instead of fixing the underlying architecture → technical debt accumulates → system becomes unmaintainable
- **Hiring contractors** instead of building internal capability → internal capability atrophies → more contractor dependence
- **Pain medication** as substitute for addressing the underlying condition
- **[Shallow Work](./shallow-work.md)** as a proxy for productive output — feels like progress, defers the deep work that would actually solve the problem

**Cross-reference:** Senge's #3 learning disability ("the illusion of taking charge") is Shifting the Burden mistaken for proactiveness.

---

### 3. Tragedy of the Commons

**Structure:** Multiple actors draw from a shared resource. Each individual's rational action degrades the resource for all. No single actor has incentive to restrain because the cost is shared and the benefit is individual.

**Behaviour:** Each actor increases their draw → total draw exceeds regeneration rate → resource degrades → eventually collapses.

**The trap:** The rational thing for each individual is to use more. The rational outcome for the group is collapse.

**Examples:**
- Overfishing, overgrazing, deforestation
- Shared infrastructure in organisations (shared databases, shared services) — each team optimises their own use at the expense of shared performance
- Shared attention in open offices — each individual takes a small slice of others' attention; total cost is catastrophic for [deep work](./deep-work.md)
- Email and Slack in knowledge work organisations

**The fix:** Either privatise the commons (each actor bears their own costs) or regulate it (imposed balancing loop). Neither is politically easy.

---

### 4. Escalation

**Structure:** Actor A increases → Actor B responds by increasing → A responds to B's increase → B responds again. Two balancing loops in competition, each treating the other's level as a threat.

**Behaviour:** Both parties escalate beyond any rational threshold. The loop runs until one party gives up, a constraint is hit, or both are destroyed.

**Examples:**
- Arms races
- Price wars
- Advertising spend wars
- Salary escalation in competitive hiring
- Retaliation cycles in conflicts

**Cross-reference:** [Reciprocity](./reciprocity.md) is the mechanism that drives escalation in social contexts. [Commitment & Consistency](./commitment-and-consistency.md) makes it hard to de-escalate once started.

---

### 5. Success to the Successful

**Structure:** Two activities compete for the same limited resource (budget, attention, talent). The one that is initially more successful gets more resources, which makes it more successful, which further concentrates resources.

**Behaviour:** Winner-takes-all dynamics. The initial advantage compounds.

**Examples:**
- [The Innovator's Dilemma](./innovators-dilemma.md): incumbents' best customers get the most investment → best products for best customers → disruptors are ignored because they serve less profitable customers
- [Amazon flywheel](./amazon-growth-flywheel.md) vs. competitors: each advantage reinforces the next
- Internal company politics: teams with more political capital get more resources → produce more visible results → get more capital
- Rich get richer; network effects; monopoly dynamics

**Cross-reference:** This archetype is driven by a [reinforcing feedback loop](./feedback-loops.md). The fix is often a balancing intervention — antitrust, portfolio diversification, deliberate investment in the underdog.

---

### 6. Eroding Goals

**Structure:** There is a gap between the goal and the current state. Instead of taking action to close the gap, the goal is lowered. The lowered goal reduces the pressure to act. The gap closes — by moving the target, not improving performance.

**Behaviour:** Standards drift downward. Quality erodes. Performance benchmarks are revised to match reality rather than reality being revised to match benchmarks.

**Examples:**
- Software quality: the team can't fix all the bugs → the acceptable bug count is raised → quality standards drift
- OKR sandbagging: if you can't hit the target, adjust the target
- "We don't need to be world-class here, just good enough" (repeated until everything is good enough)

**The fix:** Hold the goal. Treat the gap as a problem to solve, not a target to revise. [OKRs](./okrs.md) with explicit committed vs. aspirational distinctions help — but only if the committed goals are genuinely held.

---

## Recognising Archetypes

The power of naming archetypes: once you can identify the structure, you can predict the trajectory and find the right intervention.

**Questions to diagnose the archetype:**
- Is growth slowing despite more effort? → Limits to Growth (find the constraint)
- Is the quick fix being applied repeatedly? → Shifting the Burden (find the fundamental solution)
- Are multiple parties escalating past rational limits? → Escalation (is there a face-saving de-escalation path?)
- Is a shared resource degrading despite each individual acting rationally? → Tragedy of the Commons (what regulates access?)
- Are standards drifting downward? → Eroding Goals (hold the goal)
