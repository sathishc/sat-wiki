# Leverage Points

[Donella Meadows'](../people/donella-meadows.md) hierarchy of places to intervene in a system, ranked from **least to most effective**. Originally published as a 1999 essay; expanded in *Thinking in Systems* (2008).

The central insight: **most policy interventions target the bottom of this hierarchy** — changing numbers and parameters — while the highest [Leverage](./leverage.md) interventions (changing information flows, goals, and paradigms) are consistently underutilised because they are harder to see, harder to agree on, and more politically threatening.

> "Before you disturb a system, understand it." — Meadows

---

## The Twelve Leverage Points (12 = Least, 1 = Most)

### 12. Numbers (Constants and Parameters)
Sizes, rates, subsidies, taxes, standards.

These feel important but rarely change system behaviour. Changing the tax rate, the speed limit, or the interest rate tweaks the inputs — but if the fundamental feedback structure is unchanged, the system's behaviour is largely unchanged.

**Example:** Adjusting the subsidy for renewable energy (a number) won't fix an energy system built around fossil fuel infrastructure and political economy. It may shift the market slightly. It won't transform it.

**When it matters:** Numbers matter when they are near a critical threshold — a parameter that, if crossed, shifts the system into a different regime. Otherwise, they are the least powerful intervention.

---

### 11. Sizes of Stocks and Buffers
Reservoir size, inventory capacity, rainy-day funds.

Larger buffers make systems more resilient to shocks (they absorb variability without destabilising). But buffers are often physically large and expensive to change — a dam, a warehouse, a reserve currency. Slow and expensive, but sometimes the only option.

---

### 10. Structure of Material Flows
Physical layout, infrastructure, network topology.

Building or tearing down physical infrastructure changes flow structures. Very high impact when done — but extremely expensive and slow. You can't quickly reroute a supply chain.

---

### 9. Lengths of Delays
Time between action and feedback.

**High leverage.** Long delays cause oscillation and overshoot. Shortening the feedback loop allows faster learning and more precise correction.

**Applications:**
- [Batch size reduction](./batch-size.md) (Reinertsen) — smaller releases = faster feedback = less overshoot
- Weekly OKR check-ins vs. annual performance reviews
- Continuous deployment vs. quarterly software releases
- Rapid prototyping vs. year-long product cycles

Delays are often invisible — they feel like the system's natural rhythm, not a structural choice. Identifying and shortening them is often technically and organisationally feasible.

---

### 8. Strength of Balancing Feedback Loops
How powerful is the goal-seeking mechanism?

Stronger balancing loops mean the system corrects more powerfully toward its goal. Weaker loops mean the system drifts.

**Applications:**
- Regulatory strength: a strong environmental regulator corrects pollution more effectively than a weak one
- [WIP limits](./wip-limits.md): a hard WIP limit is a stronger balancing loop than a soft guideline
- [OKR scoring accountability](./okrs.md): genuine consequences for missed OKRs create stronger balancing loops

---

### 7. Gain Around Driving Positive Feedback Loops
Slowing or changing the rate of a reinforcing loop.

**High leverage.** Reinforcing loops left unchecked produce exponential growth or collapse. Slowing the gain of a destructive reinforcing loop — or accelerating a virtuous one — changes system trajectory dramatically.

**Applications:**
- Interest rates change the gain of economic reinforcing loops
- [Network effects](./amazon-growth-flywheel.md): whoever controls the gain of the dominant reinforcing loop controls the market
- Slowing viral spread (reducing the reproduction number R below 1 stops exponential growth)

---

### 6. Structure of Information Flows
**Who gets what information, when, and in what form.**

**Very high leverage.** Decisions are made based on information. Changing which information reaches which decision-maker changes decisions — without changing any rules or goals.

**Applications:**
- [Radical Transparency](./radical-transparency.md) (Dalio): making all information visible to all decision-makers changes the entire decision-making culture
- Public [OKRs](./okrs.md): making goals visible across the organisation creates alignment and accountability that private goals cannot
- Market price mechanisms: prices broadcast information about scarcity to millions of actors simultaneously
- [Seen vs Unseen](./seen-vs-unseen.md): the unseen costs of policy remain invisible to decision-makers → perverse incentives → wrong choices. Making them visible is a Level 6 intervention.

Meadows notes this is one of the most powerful and underused levers: *"Missing feedback is one of the most common causes of system malfunction. Adding or restoring information can be a powerful intervention."*

---

### 5. Rules (Incentives, Constraints, Laws)
The formal and informal rules that govern how the system operates.

**Very high leverage.** Rules define what actors can and cannot do. Changing the rules changes the game — not just the players' strategies.

**Applications:**
- Antitrust law changes market structure
- [OKRs as operating rules](./okrs.md): the rule "we only work on things in our OKRs" changes resource allocation
- [Commitment devices](./commitment-and-consistency.md): making commitments public and consequential changes the rules of the decision environment
- Constitutional rules (who can make decisions, how) shape all downstream political behaviour

---

### 4. Power to Change the Rules
Who has authority to change the rules matters as much as the rules themselves.

If rules can only be changed by those who benefit from them, the system is self-reinforcing at the rule level. The ability to change the rules is a meta-level of power.

**Applications:**
- [Idea Meritocracy](./idea-meritocracy.md) (Dalio): giving anyone with a good track record the power to challenge and change principles
- Regulatory capture: when regulated industries control the regulators, they control the rules
- [Strategic intent](./strategic-intent.md): companies that shape industry standards set the rules for everyone

---

### 3. Goals of the System
What is the system trying to achieve?

**Highest-leverage interventions.** If the goal changes, the entire balancing loop structure shifts to pursue the new goal. This is why mission and purpose matter — they literally change what the system optimises for.

**Applications:**
- Amazon's goal: "most customer-centric company" — this single goal reshapes every subsequent decision
- Bridgewater's goal: [radical truth and transparency](./radical-transparency.md) — not profit maximisation, but a specific cultural goal that shapes everything
- [OKRs](./okrs.md): the Objective is a goal statement; choosing the right goal is more important than perfecting the Key Results

---

### 2. Mindset / Paradigm
The shared assumptions, beliefs, and values from which the system arises.

**Near the highest leverage.** The paradigm is the source of the goals, the rules, the information structures. Change the paradigm and everything downstream changes.

**Examples:**
- "Growth is always good" → "wellbeing matters, not just GDP"
- "Employees are costs" → "employees are assets"
- "The market is always right" → "markets have systemic failures"
- [Mental models](./mental-models.md): the invisible assumptions that shape how decision-makers see problems

Paradigm change is slow, contested, and often only happens at crisis points — when the existing paradigm demonstrably fails.

---

### 1. Power to Transcend Paradigms
The ability to step outside the current paradigm — to hold it lightly, to choose among paradigms, to remain unattached to any single worldview.

**The highest leverage of all** — and the most rarely achieved. It requires the capacity to see that your current paradigm is a model, not reality. That there are other models. That you can choose.

Meadows calls this a stance of "rigorous empiricism, deep commitment, and light attachment."

**Application:** This is what makes scientific revolutions, genuine strategic pivots, and culture transformations possible. It's also what Dalio's [radical open-mindedness](./radical-transparency.md) is reaching toward — the ability to update your deepest operating assumptions based on evidence.

---

## The Political Paradox

The highest-leverage interventions (Goals, Paradigms, Information Flows) are also the most politically threatening. They change who has power, what the system optimises for, and what people believe. They are resisted most strongly.

Most policy and most management focuses on Level 12 (numbers) because:
- They are the easiest to quantify and agree on
- They don't threaten existing power structures
- They produce visible action (something was done)
- They rarely change system behaviour

This is why organisations keep implementing the same solutions to recurring problems and wonder why nothing changes.
