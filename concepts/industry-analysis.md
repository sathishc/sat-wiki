# Industry Analysis

## One-Line Summary
Industry structure — not managerial skill — determines the average profitability available to firms in that industry; understanding structure is the starting point of strategy.

## Porter's Core Claim
Porter's most important and most contested insight: the primary determinant of firm profitability is the attractiveness of the industry it competes in, not the quality of its management. A firm in a structurally attractive industry earns above-average returns almost by default; a firm in a structurally unattractive industry struggles even with excellent execution.

This is the "outside-in" view of strategy. You choose your battlefield before you choose your tactics.

## Structural Analysis via Five Forces
Industry structure is analysed through [Porter's Five Forces](./porters-five-forces.md):

1. **Threat of new entrants** — how easy is it for new competitors to enter?
2. **Bargaining power of suppliers** — how much can suppliers extract?
3. **Bargaining power of buyers** — how much can buyers demand?
4. **Threat of substitutes** — can buyers meet the same need differently?
5. **Competitive rivalry** — how intense is competition among existing players?

A structurally attractive industry has high entry barriers, weak suppliers, fragmented buyers, no good substitutes, and moderate rivalry. Pharmaceuticals (historically) and enterprise software are examples. Airlines and retail are examples of structurally unattractive industries.

## Dominant Force Analysis
In practice, one or two forces usually dominate a given industry. Strategic analysis should identify which force is most constraining profitability and whether and how it can be shifted:

- **Commodity industries**: buyer power and rivalry dominate — the only escape is differentiation
- **Regulated industries**: government (a quasi-force Porter added later) and barriers to entry dominate
- **Platform markets**: network effects create a new entry barrier not in Porter's original model

## Industry Evolution
Industries are not static. They evolve through predictable stages:

1. **Emergence**: low rivalry, high uncertainty, few entrants — the crossing the chasm moment (see [Crossing the Chasm](./crossing-the-chasm.md))
2. **Growth**: new entrants flood in as the market proves itself — rivalry intensifies
3. **Maturity**: consolidation, cost leadership becomes critical, differentiation harder to sustain
4. **Decline**: demand falls; rivalry becomes extreme; exit barriers trap players

Understanding where an industry sits in this evolution tells you which generic strategy applies (see [Generic Strategies](./generic-strategies.md)) and where disruption is likely to emerge.

## Disruption as Structural Change
Christensen's disruption mechanism ([Disruptive Innovation](./innovators-dilemma.md)) can be read as a Porter story: a disruptive entrant enters at the low end (cost focus) where incumbents don't bother competing, then gradually moves up-market as their technology improves, ultimately reshaping industry structure entirely. The Five Forces analysis of the original industry looked attractive to incumbents precisely because it was — which is why they ignored the disruption.

## Industry Analysis vs. Competitive Advantage
Porter's two books are complementary:
- *Competitive Strategy* (1980) — industry-level analysis; Five Forces; which industry to enter and how to position within it
- *Competitive Advantage* (1985) — firm-level analysis; [Value Chain](./value-chain.md); how to actually build and sustain a cost or differentiation advantage within the chosen position

## PESTLE as Complement
Porter's Five Forces focuses on industry economics. PESTLE (Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Legal, Environmental) is a complementary macro-environment scan — it identifies forces that may reshape industry structure over time (e.g., regulatory change as a new entry barrier, or technology as an enabler of substitutes).

## Connection to Systems Thinking
Industry structure is a system: the Five Forces are interdependent stocks and flows. A change in buyer power affects rivalry; a new entrant changes supplier dynamics. [Feedback Loops](./feedback-loops.md) operate within industries — a reinforcing loop of brand strength increasing pricing power and profitability funding more brand investment is a classic competitive moat.
