# Reciprocity

The first of [Cialdini's](../people/robert-cialdini.md) [seven principles of influence](./principles-of-influence.md). We feel obligated to return what others give us — and this obligation is one of the most universal social norms across every studied culture.

> "The rule says that we should try to repay, in kind, what another person has provided us."

## The Mechanism

When someone gives us something — a gift, a favour, a concession — we experience psychological discomfort until we reciprocate. This discomfort is the engine:

- Triggered by **uninvited** gifts, not just requested ones
- The value returned typically **exceeds** the value received
- Works asymmetrically: the giver controls timing and framing; the receiver feels the obligation

## The Door-in-the-Face Technique

A direct application of reciprocal concessions in negotiation:
1. Make a large, unreasonable request (which gets rejected)
2. "Concede" to a smaller, more reasonable request
3. The target feels obligated to reciprocate the concession — and complies

The smaller request is what you wanted all along. The large ask was setup to manufacture a concession.

## Examples

- Supermarket free samples → obligation to buy
- Hare Krishna flowers → donation pressure
- Salary negotiation: anchor high, concede to a "lower" number that's still your real target
- Corporate gifts and entertainment before contract negotiations
- Pharmaceutical company dinners for doctors before drug pitches

## Defence

Recognise when a "gift" is a compliance tool rather than genuine generosity. You can accept a gift and consciously reframe it: *"This is a sales tactic. I don't owe anything in return."* The obligation only operates when you accept its framing.
