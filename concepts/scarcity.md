# Scarcity

The sixth of [Cialdini's](../people/robert-cialdini.md) [seven principles of influence](./principles-of-influence.md). We value things more when they are rare, diminishing, or at risk of becoming unavailable. Loss is more motivating than equivalent gain.

## Two Engines of Scarcity

**1. Cognitive:** Rare things usually are more valuable. Scarcity is a genuine signal of quality and desirability — the heuristic is valid on average.

**2. Psychological reactance (Brehm's theory):** When our freedom to have something is threatened or restricted, we want it more — not because we've reassessed its value, but as a response to the threatened loss of freedom itself.

These two engines compound. Scarcity triggers both rational value reassessment and an irrational reactance response simultaneously.

## Things That *Become* Scarce

Things that **become** scarce after being plentiful generate more desire than things that were always rare. The transition matters more than the absolute level of scarcity. This is why:
- "Last 3 in stock" is more powerful than "only 3 ever made"
- Censorship of previously available information increases demand dramatically
- Price increases on familiar products feel like loss, not just new information
- Removing a product from a menu increases its perceived desirability

## Scarcity + Competition

The most potent combination. "Only 2 left — and someone else is looking at this right now" triggers both scarcity (limited supply) and [social proof](./social-proof.md) (others want it, so it must be good). Booking.com and auction platforms exploit this systematically.

## The Cookie Experiment

Identical cookies placed in near-empty jars vs. full jars: cookies in the near-empty jar rated as more desirable, more attractive, and worth paying more for. The cookie didn't change. The perceived scarcity did.

## Applications

- "Only 3 left in stock" (Amazon, Booking.com)
- Countdown timers on limited-time offers
- Flash sales, early bird pricing
- Invitation-only or waitlisted products
- Limited edition releases
- Exclusive access (members-only)

## Cross-references

**↔ [Loss Aversion](./loss-aversion.md) (Kahneman)**
Scarcity is [loss aversion](./loss-aversion.md) applied to future availability. Prospect Theory shows losses feel ~2x worse than equivalent gains feel good. "You might lose access to this" is twice as motivating as "you could gain access to this." Scarcity reframes an opportunity as an impending loss — deliberately activating loss aversion.

**↔ [Antifragility](./antifragility.md) (Taleb)**
Taleb's antifragile systems gain from volatility and disorder. Scarcity is a designed volatility signal — it creates urgency by introducing perceived instability. The antifragile response to scarcity: develop *optionality* rather than acting from urgency. Having the option to buy later is more valuable than the product itself in most scarcity scenarios.

**↔ [Compounding](./compounding.md) (Naval)**
Time under scarcity pressure is the enemy of long-term compounding thinking. Scarcity triggers short-term loss-avoidance framing that overrides long-term value assessment. Deliberate scarcity is a tool to compress decision timelines and prevent rational evaluation.

## Defence

Ask: do I actually want this, independent of the scarcity signal? If the scarcity disappeared tomorrow, would I still want it? If yes — it has genuine value. If no — you were responding to reactance, not value.

Slow down. Scarcity manipulates decision timelines. Insisting on time to think neutralises most scarcity tactics.
