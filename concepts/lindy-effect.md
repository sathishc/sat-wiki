# Lindy Effect

A heuristic from [Nassim Taleb](../people/nassim-taleb.md): **the longer something non-perishable has survived, the longer it is likely to survive**. Future life expectancy is proportional to current age.

## The Idea

For perishable things (organisms, humans), ageing is a negative signal — the older you are, the closer to death. But for non-perishable things — ideas, technologies, books, institutions, businesses — **survival is evidence of robustness**.

A book that has been in print for 100 years will likely be read for another 100. A technology that has existed for 50 years has proven its staying power in a way a 2-year-old technology has not.

## Why It Works

Non-perishable things are tested by time. Each year of survival is a stress test passed:
- Competing ideas tried to displace it and failed
- Changing conditions didn't render it obsolete
- Users and practitioners found it valuable enough to transmit

The Lindy Effect is essentially [antifragility](./antifragility.md) measured over time.

## Applications

### What to Read
- Old books that are still read are better filtered than new books
- A philosophy text from 400 BC has survived 2,400 years of intellectual challenge — higher signal than a bestseller published last month
- This is why Naval also recommends reading the classics first

### Technology Choices
- Prefer boring, proven technology over exciting new tools for critical systems
- The new framework may be better — but it hasn't been Lindy-tested
- SQL has been around since the 1970s; it will likely outlast most NoSQL alternatives

### Advice and Knowledge
- Heuristics and folk wisdom that have survived centuries often encode real information
- Academic theories that are 5 years old have lower Lindy scores than ones 50 years old

## Limits

The Lindy Effect applies to **non-perishable** things. It does not mean old = good for everything. And it can mislead when the environment changes fundamentally — a skill that was Lindy-robust in one era can become obsolete when the underlying context shifts (e.g. horse-and-buggy expertise after cars).

## Connection to Naval's [Compounding](./compounding.md)

The Lindy Effect and compounding are complementary: both reward staying power. Things that survive compound their advantage over time. Naval's advice to "read timeless books" is implicitly Lindy reasoning — the longer a book has been valuable, the more likely it continues to be.
