# Liking

The fifth of [Cialdini's](../people/robert-cialdini.md) [seven principles of influence](./principles-of-influence.md). We are more easily persuaded by people we like — and liking can be engineered through similarity, familiarity, attractiveness, flattery, and association.

## Why We Like People

Cialdini identifies five reliable drivers of liking:

| Driver | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Similarity** | Shared background, interests, values, dress, accent — signals shared values → trust |
| **Familiarity** | Mere exposure effect — the more we encounter something, the more we like it |
| **Attractiveness** | Physical attractiveness triggers halo effect across competence, intelligence, honesty |
| **Flattery** | Works even when transparently strategic — we still respond positively |
| **Association** | Being near good things (attractive people, prestigious brands, good news) transfers positive valence |

## The Mere Exposure Effect

Simply being exposed to something more often increases liking — even without any positive interaction. This is why:
- Advertising works through repetition, not just persuasion
- Familiarity breeds comfort, not contempt (in most settings)
- Local political candidates benefit enormously from name recognition alone
- The more often you see a colleague, the more you tend to like them

## Association by Proximity

We like — and dislike — the messenger, not just the message. People who deliver good news are liked; people who deliver bad news are disliked, regardless of whether the news was their doing. This is why:
- Companies use celebrities (associated with positive feelings) to sell unrelated products
- Politicians time good economic news to coincide with their appearances
- Weathercasters in Phoenix sometimes receive hate mail for reporting heat

## Sales and the Liking Trap

Sales training explicitly teaches: build rapport first, find genuine common ground, mirror the customer's posture and communication style. The goal is to create liking before the ask — so that compliance follows the liking, not the merits.

The Tupperware party model is the canonical example: you're not buying from a company rep; you're buying from a *friend*. The liking is real. The compliance trigger is deliberate.

## Cross-references

**↔ [Flow (Optimal Experience)](./flow-optimal-experience.md) (Csikszentmihalyi)**
Familiarity and rapport reduce cognitive friction — they lower the psychological cost of engagement. Environments where people feel liked and similar are more likely to produce flow states, because self-consciousness (a flow inhibitor) decreases with comfort.

**↔ [Cognitive Biases — Halo Effect](./cognitive-biases-kahneman.md)**
Physical attractiveness is a halo trigger: positive impressions in one dimension (appearance) automatically transfer to other dimensions (competence, trustworthiness). Liking and the halo effect work in tandem — attractiveness generates liking; liking generates compliance.

**↔ [Radical Transparency](./radical-transparency.md) (Dalio)**
Radical honesty requires delivering uncomfortable truths regardless of liking. Bridgewater explicitly trains people to separate the quality of an idea from the likeability of the person proposing it — and to not soften feedback because you like someone.

## Defence

Separate *liking the person* from *evaluating the offer*. Ask: would I accept this from someone I found unlikeable? If yes, proceed. If no, examine whether liking is doing the persuasive work instead of the merits.
