# Value Chain

## One-Line Summary
A firm's value chain is the sequence of activities that create the product or service — and competitive advantage comes from performing those activities differently or at lower cost than rivals.

## The Framework

Porter introduced the value chain in *Competitive Advantage* (1985) as the fundamental unit of competitive analysis. Every firm is a collection of discrete activities; margin is the difference between total value created and the total cost of performing those activities.

### Primary Activities
Activities directly involved in creating and delivering the product:

| Activity | What it covers |
|---|---|
| **Inbound logistics** | Receiving, storing, and distributing inputs (supply chain, inventory) |
| **Operations** | Transforming inputs into the final product (manufacturing, assembly) |
| **Outbound logistics** | Collecting, storing, and distributing outputs to buyers |
| **Marketing & Sales** | Communicating the value proposition and enabling purchase |
| **Service** | Installation, repair, training — maintaining and enhancing the product post-sale |

### Support Activities
Activities that enable the primary activities:

| Activity | What it covers |
|---|---|
| **Firm infrastructure** | General management, finance, legal, planning |
| **Human resource management** | Recruiting, training, compensation |
| **Technology development** | R&D, product/process innovation, IT systems |
| **Procurement** | Purchasing inputs — not just raw materials but equipment, supplies |

## Competitive Advantage Lives in the Links
The value chain's real power is not in the activities themselves but in **linkages** — how one activity affects the cost or effectiveness of another. Amazon's flywheel is a value chain linkage: lower prices → more traffic → more sellers → better selection → lower fulfilment cost → lower prices again (see [Amazon Growth Flywheel](./amazon-growth-flywheel.md)).

Linkages also extend **across** value chains — supplier linkages (how your supplier's value chain affects your cost) and buyer linkages (how your value chain fits into the buyer's). This is why vertical integration and supplier relationships are strategic choices, not just operational ones.

## Value Chain as Cost Map
The value chain disaggregates the firm's total cost into its component activities, revealing:
- **Where cost is actually incurred** (not just what the P&L shows)
- **Where value is actually added** (what the buyer is actually paying for)
- **Where margin is being squeezed** (activities with high cost but low perceived value)

This maps naturally onto [Cost of Delay](./cost-of-delay.md) thinking: every activity in the chain has an economic cost if delayed, and [Flow Economics](./flow-economics.md) asks where in the chain you are optimising for utilisation vs. for throughput.

## Industry Value Chain
Porter's broader concept: the **industry value chain** (sometimes called the value system) links together the value chains of suppliers → firm → channels → buyers. Competitive advantage can come from optimising at any link. Bezos understood that controlling more of the industry value chain (AWS → Marketplace → Logistics → Retail) lowered cost at every stage.

## Connection to Systems Thinking
A value chain is a system: activities are stocks, material and information flow between them are flows, and delays in one stage propagate downstream. [System Archetypes](./system-archetypes.md) like "shifting the burden" can occur when a downstream activity compensates for upstream defects rather than fixing the root cause.

## Relationship to Core Competency
[Core competency](./core-competency.md) (Prahalad & Hamel) and value chain are complementary lenses. Porter asks: *where in the chain do we create competitive advantage?* Prahalad asks: *which activities reflect durable capabilities that transfer across markets?* The tension is productive — value chain is an industry-level, outside-in view; core competency is a firm-level, inside-out view.
