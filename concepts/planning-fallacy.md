# Planning Fallacy

Proposed by [Kahneman](../people/daniel-kahneman.md) and Tversky (1979). The systematic tendency to **underestimate time, cost, and risk of future tasks, while overestimating their benefits** — even when the person knows similar tasks have run over in the past.

> "The planning fallacy is that you make a plan, which is usually a best-case scenario. Then you assume that the outcome will follow the plan."

## The Core Finding

In study after study, across domains from student thesis completion to large infrastructure projects:

- Predicted completion times are optimistic
- Actual completion times are significantly longer
- **The bias persists even when people are explicitly told their predictions have been optimistic before**
- Professional experts are not immune — often worse, because overconfidence increases with expertise

Classic data point: a survey of home renovation projects found the average overrun was 100%. Sydney Opera House: estimated at AUD $7M in 1957, completed in 1973 at AUD $102M.

## Why It Happens: The Inside View

Kahneman's diagnosis: **the inside view**. When planning, people construct a specific, detailed scenario of how the project will unfold. They imagine success. They don't adequately imagine failure modes, unexpected obstacles, or the base rate of similar projects.

This is [System 1](./system-1-and-2.md) constructing a coherent narrative (WYSIATI — What You See Is All There Is). The plan is vivid and concrete; the failure scenarios are vague and suppressed.

## The Fix: Reference Class Forecasting (Outside View)

Kahneman's prescription: **ignore your specific plan and look at base rates**.

1. Identify a reference class — projects similar to yours
2. Find the statistical distribution of outcomes for that class (how long did they take? how much did they cost? what fraction succeeded?)
3. Anchor your estimate to that distribution
4. Only then adjust for specific features of your project

The outside view is almost always more accurate than the inside view. It is also psychologically uncomfortable — it requires treating your project as a statistic rather than a unique, special case.

## The Pre-Mortem

A practical System 2 tool for combating the planning fallacy:

**Before committing to a plan**, imagine it is 12 months from now and the project has failed spectacularly. Write down all the reasons it failed.

This reframes the task: instead of defending the plan (System 1's default), you are tasked with attacking it. The pre-mortem surfaces risks that optimism bias suppresses.

## Cross-References

**[Reinertsen](../people/donald-reinertsen.md)'s [Cost of Delay](./cost-of-delay.md):**
The planning fallacy makes [batch size](./batch-size.md) problems worse. Teams underestimate how long large batches take, so they don't feel the urgency to break them down. Quantifying Cost of Delay forces an outside-view reckoning with time.

**[Taleb](../people/nassim-taleb.md)'s [Black Swan](./black-swan.md):**
The planning fallacy is the planning-level manifestation of Black Swan blindness — the refusal to adequately model the tail. Projects don't just run 10% over; they sometimes fail catastrophically. Inside-view planning ignores the distribution's fat tail.

**[Dalio](../people/ray-dalio.md)'s [Five-Step Process](./five-step-process.md):**
"Diagnose root causes" before designing is a discipline against inside-view planning. Dalio also stress-tests assumptions against historical data — the outside view applied to investment theses.

**[Amazon LP #9 Bias for Action](./amazon-leadership-principles.md):**
The tension: bias for action (move fast) can amplify planning fallacy (underestimate what you're getting into). Amazon's Type 1/2 decision distinction is the resolution — move fast on reversible decisions, deliberate on irreversible ones.
