# Disruptive Innovation

The central concept from Clayton Christensen's *The Innovator's Dilemma* (1997). Disruptive innovation explains why **well-managed companies following best practices still lose to new entrants** — and why this failure is rational at every step.

## The Core Mechanism

Successful incumbents are embedded in value networks — customers, investors, cost structures — that make it rational to:
1. Listen to their best customers
2. Invest in sustaining innovation (making existing products better)
3. Ignore low-end or fringe markets with thin margins

Disruptors enter precisely those ignored spaces with simpler, cheaper, "good enough" products. Incumbents have no rational incentive to respond. Then disruptors improve — and eventually performance exceeds what mainstream customers need. At that point, the incumbent has been undercut from below.

> The dilemma: rational decisions at every step produce an irrational aggregate outcome.

## Sustaining vs. Disruptive Innovation

| | Sustaining | Disruptive |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | Better products for existing customers | Simpler/cheaper for new or low-end customers |
| Who wins | Incumbents | New entrants |
| Examples | Faster hard drives, better smartphones | Personal computers vs. mainframes, Netflix vs. Blockbuster, Honda Supercub vs. Harley |

## The Three Mechanisms of Failure

**1. Value network lock-in**
Companies are embedded in networks (customers, suppliers, investors) that punish deviation. A 14" disk drive manufacturer's entire cost structure, sales force, and customer base was built around 14" drives — making it impossible to profitably serve the 8" market.

**2. Resource allocation**
Middle managers kill disruptive projects because they don't fit existing metrics or customer demands. The people doing the killing are often right — from the perspective of current margin and current customers, the disruptive project is a bad investment. The problem is systemic, not individual.

**3. Overshooting**
Incumbents keep improving products beyond what most customers actually need. This creates headroom for a "good enough" alternative to enter below.

## Classic Examples

- **Disk drives:** Each generation (14" → 8" → 5.25" → 3.5") was led by a new entrant, never the incumbent. The incumbent always had the technology — they just couldn't serve the new market profitably.
- **Steel minimills:** Entered at cheap, low-margin rebar; integrated mills were happy to cede it. Minimills improved and moved up to structural steel, then sheet steel — eventually destroying the integrated mills.
- **Honda (motorcycles):** Entered the US market with small 50cc Supercubs for people who had never ridden. Harley and BSA ignored them. Honda improved and eventually served the whole market.
- **Digital cameras vs. film**
- **Netflix vs. Blockbuster** — DVD mail first, then streaming
- **PCs vs. mainframes and minicomputers**

## The Overshooting Trajectory

```
Performance
    │           ┌──── Incumbent trajectory (keeps improving)
    │          /
    │    ─────/ ◄─── Customer needs (grows slower than product improves)
    │   /
    │  / ◄─── Disruptor enters here (below customer needs)
    └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────► Time
```

Once the disruptor's trajectory intersects mainstream customer needs, the incumbent's performance advantage is irrelevant — the disruptor wins on price/simplicity/accessibility.

## Why Incumbents Can't Respond

The standard response — "just create a skunkworks" — usually fails. The parent company's:
- **Values** determine investment allocation (disruptive opportunities look small and marginal)
- **Processes** are optimised for existing customers and margins
- **Sales force** won't sell lower-margin products

See [RPM Framework](../concepts/innovators-solution.md) for why internal disruption responses fail.

The prescription: create a **separate autonomous organisation** with its own P&L, customers, and cost structure — one that can legitimately succeed at the lower margin of the disruptive market.

## New-Market vs. Low-End Disruption

Christensen identifies two types:
- **New-market disruption:** Targets non-consumers — people who couldn't use existing solutions (too expensive, too complex). E.g., PCs targeting people who couldn't afford minicomputers.
- **Low-end disruption:** Targets overserved customers at the low end of an existing market. E.g., discount retailers, steel minimills.

Both follow the same trajectory: enter at the neglected fringe, improve, move up-market.
