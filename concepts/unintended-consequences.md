# Unintended Consequences

A pattern that appears throughout [Hazlitt](../people/henry-hazlitt.md), [Taleb](../people/nassim-taleb.md), [Dalio](../people/ray-dalio.md), and [Reinertsen](../people/donald-reinertsen.md): **interventions in complex systems routinely produce effects opposite to those intended**, because the system responds in ways the intervener didn't model.

> "The curious task of economics is to demonstrate to men how little they really know about what they imagine they can design."
> — Friedrich Hayek (Hazlitt's intellectual peer)

## The Structure

Every unintended consequence follows the same pattern:

1. **Intervention** — a policy or action designed to produce outcome X
2. **First-order effect** — outcome X occurs (the seen)
3. **System response** — the complex system adjusts in ways not anticipated
4. **Second-order effect** — outcome Y (often opposite of X) emerges (the unseen)
5. **Attribution error** — the intervener takes credit for X, blames external factors for Y

## Examples from Hazlitt

| Intervention | Intended Effect | Unintended Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Rent control | Affordable housing | Housing shortage, deteriorating stock |
| Price ceilings on goods | Affordable necessities | Shortages, black markets |
| Tariffs | Protect domestic jobs | Higher consumer prices, retaliatory tariffs, fewer export jobs |
| Minimum wage | Higher incomes for workers | Unemployment for least-skilled workers |
| Inflation | Stimulate the economy | Distorted prices, redistribution from savers to debtors |
| Farm price supports | Help farmers | Overproduction, consumer costs, agricultural inefficiency |

## The Root Cause

Unintended consequences arise from the same cognitive error as the [Broken Window Fallacy](./broken-window-fallacy.md): **reasoning about a subset of effects in a system that produces all effects simultaneously**.

Complex systems (economies, organisations, ecosystems) have feedback loops, substitutions, and adaptations that simple linear models don't capture. When you change one variable, the system adjusts everything else.

## Cross-Domain Parallels

**Taleb — [Antifragility](./antifragility.md):**
Naive intervention suppresses small volatility (seen: stability) while building up hidden fragility (unseen: catastrophic risk). The intended effect is safety; the unintended consequence is brittleness.

> "The tragedy of modernity: those trying to help are often hurting us the most."

**Reinertsen — [Queues](./queues-in-product-development.md):**
Maximise resource utilisation (intended: efficiency) → queues explode (unintended: slow delivery and poor quality). The intervention to "improve efficiency" destroys flow.

**Dalio — [Five-Step Process](./five-step-process.md):**
Dalio's insistence on diagnosing root causes before designing solutions is precisely the discipline of asking: what are the second-order effects of this intervention?

**Amazon — [LP #9 Bias for Action](./amazon-leadership-principles.md):**
Amazon's distinction between Type 1 (irreversible) and Type 2 (reversible) decisions is a framework for managing unintended consequences: be cautious about irreversible interventions; be bold about reversible ones.

## The Lesson

**Before any intervention:**
1. What is the system I am intervening in?
2. What are the first-order effects? (The seen)
3. What are the second- and third-order effects? (The unseen)
4. Who bears costs that have no advocate?
5. Is this decision reversible? (If not, apply much higher scrutiny)

This is the Hazlitt/Bastiat question generalised as a thinking discipline.
