# Output vs Activity

**Origin:** Andy Grove, *High Output Management* (1983); related to Cal Newport's *Deep Work* and Donald Reinertsen's flow economics

## Core Idea

Being busy is not the same as being productive. A manager who works 60 hours a week, attends every meeting, and responds to every email is not necessarily doing good management — they may be performing activity while producing little output.

Grove's formulation is direct: **managers are measured by output, not effort**. The question is not "did I work hard?" but "did my team produce more because of how I spent my time?"

This is a fundamentally different orientation from most professional environments, where visible effort is rewarded and output is assumed to follow from it.

## The Distinction

| Activity | Output |
|---|---|
| Attending a meeting | A decision made, a blocker removed, a team aligned |
| Reviewing a document | The document improved; the author's thinking sharpened |
| Sending many emails | Problems solved; information routed correctly |
| Being "always available" | People unstuck; confidence maintained |

The activity is the *input*. The output is what changed as a result. Managers who optimise for activity — staying busy, looking engaged, filling their calendar — often crowd out the high-leverage work that actually moves things.

## Why Activity Gets Rewarded Anyway

The activity trap has structural causes:

- **Visibility:** Activity is observable; output is often delayed, diffuse, and hard to attribute
- **Busyness as status:** In many organisations, being busy signals importance
- **Short-term comfort:** Responding to every message *feels* productive; deep strategic thinking feels uncomfortable and unverifiable
- **Measurement difficulty:** Team output is hard to measure; hours worked or meetings attended are easy

Grove's insight is that these incentives systematically push managers toward low-leverage, high-visibility work and away from high-leverage, low-visibility work like developing people, removing systemic blockers, and making good decisions.

## The Manager's Production Function

Grove frames the manager's job as a production process. The manager has inputs (time, attention, information) and must convert them into outputs (team performance, decisions, capability). Like any production process, you can optimise it:

- Identify the **bottleneck**: where is team output most constrained?
- Focus on **leverage**: which activities multiply the output of many people?
- Reduce **waste**: which activities consume time without improving team output?

This framing connects directly to **[Managerial Leverage](managerial-leverage.md)** and to Reinertsen's **[Flow Economics](flow-economics.md)**, which applies a similar throughput lens to product development.

## Connection to Deep Work

Cal Newport's **[Deep Work](deep-work.md)** makes a complementary argument at the individual level: cognitively demanding, high-value work requires sustained concentration and is destroyed by the constant context-switching that "being available" demands.

The output-vs-activity distinction applies here: a manager who protects four hours of uninterrupted thinking time may produce more valuable output than one who spends the same four hours in reactive mode — even though the reactive manager *looks* busier.

Newport and Grove agree: the appearance of productivity is not productivity.

## Implications for Self-Assessment

Grove's framework implies a daily audit question: *What output did I produce today?* Not: *what did I do?*

Concretely:
- What decisions were made that would not have been made without me?
- What blockers were removed?
- What capability did I build in my team?
- What information was routed correctly that would otherwise have been lost?

If the answer is consistently "not much," the calendar needs to change — regardless of how full it was.
