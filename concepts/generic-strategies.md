# Generic Strategies

Introduced by [Michael Porter](../people/michael-porter.md) in *Competitive Strategy* (1980). There are three internally consistent approaches — "generic strategies" — through which a firm can achieve sustainable competitive advantage. Every successful competitive position is a variant of one of these three. Firms that fail to choose clearly among them are "stuck in the middle" and earn below-average returns.

> "Strategy is about making choices, trade-offs; it's about deliberately choosing to be different." — Porter

## The Three Generic Strategies

### 1. Overall Cost Leadership

Achieve the **lowest delivered cost** in the industry while maintaining acceptable quality and value.

**What it requires:**
- Aggressive investment in scale and efficient facilities
- Vigorous pursuit of cost reductions from experience
- Tight overhead cost control
- Avoiding marginal customer accounts
- Cost minimisation in all functional areas (R&D, service, sales force, advertising)

**Competitive logic**: A low-cost position defends against all five forces. It means:
- Higher margins even when rivals cut prices (rivalry defence)
- More cushion when suppliers raise input prices (supplier power defence)
- More flexibility on price to deter entry (entry barrier)
- More room to match substitute products on price (substitute defence)
- Ability to serve price-sensitive buyers that rivals cannot profitably serve (buyer power defence)

**Risks of cost leadership:**
- Technological change that nullifies past investment or learning
- Imitation by new entrants with modern equipment (late-mover advantage)
- Inability to see the required product or marketing changes because of focus on cost
- Cost inflation that narrows the firm's lead over differentiated competitors
- Disruptors who enter via an even lower-cost approach targeting non-consumers

### 2. Differentiation

Create something **perceived industry-wide as unique** and command a premium price that exceeds the cost of differentiating.

Differentiation can take many forms: product design or brand image (BMW), technology (Apple), features (Mercedes), customer service (Nordstrom), dealer network, or simply being the first to solve a problem in a distinctive way.

**What it requires:**
- Strong marketing abilities
- Product engineering talent
- Creative flair and strong R&D
- Corporate reputation for quality or technological leadership
- Long history in the industry or a unique set of skills
- Strong co-operation from channels

**Competitive logic**: Differentiation protects against competitive forces differently from cost leadership:
- Buyer loyalty reduces price sensitivity; buyers will pay a premium (rivalry defence)
- Higher margins provide cushion against supplier cost increases (supplier power defence)
- Customer loyalty creates entry barrier (entry defence)
- Loyal customers are less likely to switch to substitutes (substitute defence)
- A premium price means the firm doesn't need to compete with every buyer (buyer selectivity)

**Risks of differentiation:**
- The cost differential between cost leaders and differentiated firms becomes too large — buyers sacrifice features for price
- Buyers' need for the differentiating factor falls (tastes change, information improves)
- Imitation narrows perceived differentiation (especially in technology)
- The differentiation advantage is in features buyers don't actually value highly

### 3. Focus

Concentrate on a **specific buyer group, product segment, or geographic market** and serve it better than any broadly targeted competitor can.

Focus is built on the premise that the firm can serve its narrow target more effectively or efficiently than competitors who serve more broadly. It achieves either a differentiation focus (serve the target better) or a cost focus (serve the target more cheaply).

**Focus targets:**
- Specific buyer groups (age cohort, usage occasion, income bracket)
- Specific product segment (product line subset, configuration)
- Specific geographic market

**Competitive logic**: A focused firm gains competitive advantage by optimising its activities for its specific target. Broadly targeted rivals inevitably make compromises to serve many segments; the focused firm has no such compromises.

**Risks of focus strategy:**
- The cost differential between the focused firm and broad competitors narrows
- Differences between the target segment and the broader market narrow over time
- Competitors find sub-segments within the focused firm's target to focus on even more narrowly

## "Stuck in the Middle"

Porter's most-quoted warning: **firms that try to be all things to all customers end up achieving no strategic advantage at all**.

A firm stuck in the middle:
- Has no cost advantage (because it didn't invest single-mindedly in cost reduction)
- Has no meaningful differentiation (because it didn't invest in the capabilities that create unique value)
- Has no focus advantage (because it tries to serve everyone)

Result: **below-average industry returns**, even in a structurally attractive industry.

The stuck-in-the-middle trap is the organisational equivalent of trying to play two incompatible sports simultaneously. Cost leadership requires standardisation, process discipline, overhead minimisation. Differentiation requires investment in features, R&D, brand, and service. The operational requirements of each strategy conflict. Trying to pursue both without exceptional strategic architecture simply means doing neither well.

## Bowman's Strategy Clock (Extension)

Cliff Bowman extended Porter's framework with the "Strategy Clock" — a richer map of competitive positioning that shows eight strategic positions plotted on axes of perceived value (to buyer) vs. price:

| Position | Low price | High value | Description |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Low | Low | Low value/low price — viable only in very price-sensitive segments |
| 2 | Low | Medium | Cost leadership — price advantage with acceptable quality |
| 3 | Medium | High | Differentiation — premium value at premium price |
| 4 | Low | High | Hybrid — high value at low price; difficult to sustain |
| 5-8 | Various | Low | Failure strategies — high price with low perceived value |

The clock clarifies that "stuck in the middle" (position 4 attempted without capability) is not the same as a genuine "hybrid" strategy (position 4 achieved by exceptional operational excellence and scale), which very few firms can sustain.

## Application: Amazon and the Hybrid Exception

[Amazon](../people/jeff-bezos.md) appears to violate Porter's "stuck in the middle" warning — it simultaneously pursues cost leadership (lowest prices, efficient logistics) and differentiation (Prime convenience, Kindle ecosystem, AWS scale). How?

1. **Sequencing**: Amazon started with pure cost leadership (cheapest books), built scale, then used that scale to invest in differentiation (Prime, Alexa, AWS). It is not simultaneously pursuing both from a standing start.

2. **Separate businesses**: AWS is a differentiated B2B cloud services business. Amazon retail is a cost leader. Amazon Prime is a differentiation play. These are distinct P&L-level strategic positions, not a single undifferentiated strategy.

3. **The [Amazon Growth Flywheel](./amazon-growth-flywheel.md)**: The flywheel creates a reinforcing loop where scale enables cost reduction enables lower prices enables more customers enables more scale. At sufficient scale, cost leadership and differentiation become mutually reinforcing rather than mutually exclusive.

Porter's own *What Is Strategy?* (1996) addressed this: sustainable "outperforming" requires **strategic fit** — the activities of the whole firm must reinforce each other. Amazon's activities do reinforce each other. Firms that try to mix strategies without this systemic fit get stuck in the middle.

## Application: Christensen's Disruptors and Cost Focus

[Clayton Christensen](../people/clayton-christensen.md)'s disruptors almost always enter via a **cost focus** strategy:
- They target a non-consuming or low-end segment
- They offer a simpler, cheaper product than incumbents
- Incumbents are happy to cede the low-margin segment
- The disruptor improves along the performance trajectory

This is not a violation of generic strategies — it is a perfect illustration of cost focus. The disruption occurs when the disruptor exits the focus position and expands up-market, eventually competing for mainstream customers with a still-lower cost structure.
