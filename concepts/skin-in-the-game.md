# Skin in the Game

A principle from [Nassim Taleb](../people/nassim-taleb.md): **those who make decisions must bear the consequences of those decisions**. Accountability requires exposure to your own recommendations.

## The Core Idea

Systems become fragile when decision-makers are insulated from the consequences of their choices. When bankers, politicians, consultants, or advisors can make recommendations that benefit themselves while the downside falls on others, you get:
- Asymmetric risk (others bear it)
- Moral hazard (no incentive to be right)
- Systemic fragility (bad decisions propagate unchecked)

The solution is simple: you must have skin in the game. If you recommend it, you must be exposed to it going wrong.

## Historical Roots

Taleb traces this principle back to Hammurabi's Code (~1750 BC): if a builder constructs a house that collapses and kills the owner, the builder is put to death. Harsh — but it perfectly aligns incentives. The builder has maximum skin in the game.

## Examples of Missing Skin in the Game

- **Financial advisors** paid on commissions regardless of client outcomes
- **Economists** whose forecasts are never audited against reality
- **Politicians** who send others to wars they would never fight
- **Consultants** who recommend restructuring and then leave
- **Doctors** who over-prescribe because their income doesn't depend on patient outcomes

## Connection to Naval's [Accountability](../people/naval-ravikant.md)

This directly overlaps with Naval Ravikant's concept of taking on accountability:

> "Embrace accountability and take business risks under your own name. Society will reward you with responsibility, equity, and leverage."

Both argue the same thing from different angles:
- **Taleb:** Systems are healthier when participants bear consequences
- **Naval:** Individuals gain leverage by voluntarily taking on accountability

The entrepreneur who puts their name on a business, risks their own capital, and bears the consequences of failure is the canonical skin-in-the-game actor — and, not coincidentally, the one who can build the most [leverage](./leverage.md).

## Via Negativa

Taleb extends this into an epistemological principle: **via negativa** — it is often easier and more reliable to know what to remove than what to add. The doctor who refrains from intervening when the body can heal itself; the investor who avoids bad bets rather than seeking good ones; the manager who removes bureaucracy rather than adding processes.

> "Subtraction is often more powerful than addition."

This echoes Naval's happiness framework: [happiness](./happiness.md) comes from removing desires, not adding achievements.
