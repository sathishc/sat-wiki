# Task-Relevant Maturity

**Origin:** Andy Grove, *High Output Management* (1983)

## Core Idea

**Task-Relevant Maturity** (TRM) is Grove's framework for matching a manager's style to the readiness of an employee for a *specific task*. It is not a fixed property of a person — it is a function of a person's experience, knowledge, and motivation relative to a particular piece of work.

The manager who manages every person the same way, regardless of their TRM, is doing it wrong — either smothering capable people with unnecessary oversight, or abandoning inexperienced people who need direction.

## The Three Modes

| TRM Level | Management Style | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **Low** | Directive | Tell them what to do, how to do it, and check frequently. This is not micromanagement — it is appropriate support for someone who does not yet have the tools to succeed independently. |
| **Medium** | Coaching | Share the reasoning, invite input, explain the why. The employee has some capability but needs support to develop judgment. The manager is a thought partner, not a giver of orders. |
| **High** | Delegating | Set the objective, agree on key results, get out of the way. Intervene only if asked or if monitoring reveals something off-track. |

## Key Insight: TRM is Task-Specific

A senior engineer with 20 years of experience has **high TRM** for engineering problems and may have **low TRM** for their first people management responsibility. Treating them with a delegating style on the management task — because they are "senior" — will lead to failure.

This is the most common management error: using a person's general seniority or status as a proxy for their readiness on a specific task.

## The Manager's Error Modes

Grove identifies two failure modes:

1. **Under-managing (false delegation):** Applying delegating style to a low-TRM person. The employee fails, feels abandoned, and the manager is surprised. "I gave them the autonomy to do it their way" is not an excuse if the person didn't yet have the capability to succeed.

2. **Over-managing (micromanagement):** Applying directive style to a high-TRM person. The employee feels mistrusted, their motivation drops, and their capability atrophies. This is often done by managers who are anxious about outcomes rather than calibrated to their team.

## TRM Changes Over Time

As an employee gains experience on a task, their TRM rises — and the manager must adjust style accordingly. This requires **ongoing calibration**: actively assessing where each person is and being willing to shift mode even when the previous mode felt comfortable.

The manager who drifts into a fixed style — always coaching, always directive, always hands-off — has stopped doing the work of managing.

## Connection to Leverage

TRM is not just about the individual employee — it is about **[managerial leverage](managerial-leverage.md)**. Mismatched management style is low-[Leverage](leverage.md): either wasted manager time on unnecessary oversight, or wasted potential from employees who could be doing more if trusted to do so.

Getting TRM calibration right is one of the highest-leverage things a manager can do consistently across their team.

## Practical Application

- **At the start of a new task or role:** Default to more structured support than you think is needed. It is easier to pull back than to recover from a preventable failure.
- **When something goes wrong:** Ask whether the failure was a capability problem (low TRM — needs more support) or a motivation problem (different intervention).
- **In one-on-ones:** Explicitly discuss TRM with the employee — "where do you feel confident on this, and where do you want more input?"
