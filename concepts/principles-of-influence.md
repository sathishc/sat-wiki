# Principles of Influence (Cialdini)

[Robert Cialdini's](../people/robert-cialdini.md) seven principles from *Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion* (1984, expanded 2021). The framework explains why human beings comply with requests — and how that compliance is triggered, ethically or otherwise.

The core insight: **humans use cognitive shortcuts to decide when to comply**. These shortcuts — heuristics — evolved because they usually produce good outcomes. They become exploitable when the trigger is present without the underlying validity.

Cialdini calls them **click-whirr responses**: press the right trigger, and the behaviour plays automatically.

---

## The Seven Principles

### 1. Reciprocity

> We feel obligated to return what others give us.

**Why it works:** The rule of reciprocity is one of the most universal social norms across every studied culture — deeply embedded because societies that enforced it were more cooperative and more successful.

**The mechanism:**
- A gift, favour, or concession creates psychological discomfort until reciprocated
- The obligation is triggered even by *uninvited* gifts
- The value returned typically exceeds the value received
- Works powerfully with **concessions**: if someone makes a large ask and then "concedes" to a smaller one, you feel obligated to concede too — the **door-in-the-face** technique

**Real-world examples:**
- Free samples at supermarkets
- Hare Krishna's flower-then-ask model
- Salary negotiation: anchor high, then "concede" to a lower-but-still-good number

**Defence:** Recognise when a gift is a compliance tool. Accept genuine generosity without manufactured obligation. Reframe: "this is a sales tactic, not a personal gift."

---

### 2. Commitment and Consistency

> Once we commit to a position, we feel pressure to remain consistent with it.

**Why it works:** Consistency is valued socially and personally. Inconsistency signals unreliability. We use our past behaviour as evidence about our values — "I did X, so I must be the kind of person who does X."

**The mechanism:**
- Small initial commitments prime larger subsequent ones — the **foot-in-the-door** technique
- Written commitments are more binding than verbal
- Public commitments are more binding than private
- Commitments that required effort feel more binding (cognitive dissonance reduction)
- The **lowball technique**: agree to a deal, then change the terms — people often proceed because they've already committed mentally

**Why writing matters:** When you write something down, it becomes external — a piece of evidence about who you are. The self aligns with the written record.

**Real-world examples:**
- Car dealers: "write me your best offer" — you're now committed
- Fundraisers: "do you care about children's welfare?" (yes) → "would you give $5?"
- Gym contracts: signing up makes you more likely to go, not just because you paid, but because you've declared an identity

**Defence:** Ask: *if I hadn't already committed, would I make this decision today?* Separate the original commitment from the current choice.

---

### 3. Social Proof

> When uncertain, we look to what others are doing — especially similar others.

**Why it works:** Social proof is a genuine information shortcut. If most people in a situation are doing something, it's probably the right thing to do. The heuristic fails when the crowd is wrong, or when the proof is manufactured.

**The mechanism:**
- Under uncertainty, others' behaviour is informative data
- Similar others are more influential than dissimilar ones
- Large numbers are more persuasive than small ones
- **Pluralistic ignorance:** everyone privately doubts, but assumes others are certain — producing a false consensus
- **Bystander effect:** the more people present in an emergency, the less likely any individual will help — each assumes someone else has acted

**Manufactured social proof:**
- Fake reviews and ratings
- Laugh tracks on TV
- Seeded tip jars
- "Flying off shelves" claims

**Real-world examples:**
- "9 out of 10 dentists..."
- Download counts, follower numbers
- Social media engagement metrics as proxies for quality

**Defence:** Ask: are these people genuinely similar to me? Is this proof real or manufactured? Evaluate the underlying claim, not the crowd.

---

### 4. Authority

> We defer to people with expertise or the symbols of authority.

**Why it works:** Experts usually do know more than we do. Deference to authority is cognitively efficient. The heuristic fails when symbols substitute for genuine expertise, or when real expertise in one domain is treated as authority in another.

**The mechanism:**
- **Symbols** of authority (titles, uniforms, expensive cars) trigger the same compliance as genuine authority
- True expertise in one domain is often incorrectly extended to unrelated domains (celebrity endorsements)
- The [halo effect](./cognitive-biases-kahneman.md): authority in one dimension transfers to perceived authority in others
- Genuine experts often signal credibility by **acknowledging weaknesses** — this increases trust, not decreases it

**Classic evidence:**
- Milgram experiments: ordinary people administered what they believed were lethal shocks because a lab-coated experimenter told them to
- Nurses complying with clearly incorrect medication orders from "doctors" (even fake ones by phone)

**Defence:** Ask: is this person actually an expert in *this specific domain*? Are the credentials genuine? Does their authority come with accountability (skin in the game)?

---

### 5. Liking

> We comply more easily with people we like.

**Why it works:** Liking is a reasonable proxy for trust — people similar to us probably share our values; familiar people are predictable. The heuristic breaks down when liking is engineered independent of actual trustworthiness.

**We like people who are:**
- **Similar** to us — background, interests, values, dress, accent
- **Familiar** — mere exposure effect; familiarity breeds liking (not contempt)
- **Attractive** — physical attractiveness triggers positive halo across competence, honesty, intelligence
- **Complimentary** — flattery works even when transparently strategic
- **Associated with positive things** — the messenger effect; being near good things transfers their valence

**Real-world examples:**
- Sales training: mirror and match the customer; find common ground
- Tupperware parties: you're buying from a friend
- Celebrity endorsements: liking transfers from person to product
- Good cop/bad cop: liking the good cop makes you cooperative

**Defence:** Separate *liking the person* from *evaluating the offer*. Ask: would I accept this if an unlikeable stranger made the same offer?

---

### 6. Scarcity

> We value things more when they are rare or disappearing.

**Why it works:** Scarcity is a genuine signal of value — rare things usually are more valuable. Plus: **reactance** (Brehm) — when our freedom to have something is threatened, desire intensifies.

**The mechanism:**
- Things that *become* scarce are more desired than things that were always rare — the transition matters
- Scarcity + competition is the most potent combination ("3 left, and someone else is looking")
- **Loss framing** outperforms gain framing — "what you'll lose by not acting" > "what you'll gain by acting"

**Applications:**
- "Only 3 left in stock" (Amazon, Booking.com)
- Limited-time offers, countdown clocks
- Invitation-only products
- "Last day of sale"

**The cookie experiment:** Identical cookies rated more desirable when in a near-empty jar vs. a full jar. The content didn't change; the perceived scarcity did.

**Censorship backlash:** Restricting previously available information makes it more desired than if it had never been available. Reactance explains why bans backfire.

**Defence:** Ask: do I *actually* want this? Or am I reacting to the threatened loss? If the scarcity disappeared, would I still want it?

---

### 7. Unity

> We are strongly influenced by those we perceive as *us*.

**Why it works:** We extend trust, effort, and generosity to in-group members that we withhold from out-group members. This goes beyond liking (about the individual) — Unity is about shared **identity**.

**Sources of Unity:**
- Family and kinship — the most powerful
- Shared place — hometown, neighbourhood, alma mater, nation
- Shared experience — having gone through the same thing
- **Co-creation** — being involved in making something together

**The "ask for advice" technique:**
Asking someone for their *advice* (not opinion, not help — *advice*) transforms the relationship. They become invested in the outcome. They feel ownership. Unity is created through the act of being consulted.

**Real-world examples:**
- Alumni networks: we help fellow alumni we've never met
- Military bonds: shared experience creates deep in-group loyalty
- Brand communities: Apple users, CrossFit members — shared identity, not just shared product
- Political tribes: "one of us" vs. "one of them" as a compliance trigger

**Defence:** Ask: is this "we" genuine or manufactured? Evaluate the offer on its merits, separately from group membership.

## The Unified Structure

All seven principles share the same architecture:

```
Genuine signal (usually valid)
    ↓
Cognitive shortcut (heuristic)
    ↓
Automatic compliance response
```

| Principle | Genuine signal | When it fails |
|---|---|---|
| Reciprocity | Cooperation requires mutual exchange | Uninvited gifts; manufactured obligations |
| Commitment | Consistency signals reliability | Sunk cost; identity lock-in |
| Social Proof | Crowds are usually right | Pluralistic ignorance; manufactured consensus |
| Authority | Experts usually know more | Symbols without expertise; domain mismatch |
| Liking | Similar/familiar = probably trustworthy | Engineered rapport; halo effect |
| Scarcity | Rare = usually valuable | Artificial scarcity; reactance exploitation |
| Unity | In-group = aligned interests | Manufactured identity; tribalism exploitation |

---

## Ethical Use vs. Manipulation

Cialdini's explicit position: the same principles can be used ethically or not.

**Ethical use:** Help people recognise genuine value they might miss due to their own heuristics. Use reciprocity to build real relationships; use social proof to help people find relevant evidence; use commitment to help people achieve their own goals.

**Manipulation:** Trigger compliance for something not in the target's genuine interest.

The ethical distinction also has practical stakes: manipulation produces short-term compliance and long-term trust destruction. Ethical influence produces durable relationships and repeated compliance.

## Cross-Framework Connections

### ↔ Kahneman (System 1/2 + Cognitive Biases)

Cialdini's principles are the **exploitation manual for Kahneman's [System 1](./system-1-and-2.md)**:

| Cialdini Principle | Kahneman mechanism |
|---|---|
| Social Proof | [WYSIATI](./cognitive-biases-kahneman.md) + availability heuristic |
| Authority | Halo effect |
| Scarcity | [Loss aversion](./loss-aversion.md) |
| Commitment | Anchoring + consistency bias |
| Liking | Affect heuristic |
| Reciprocity | In-group fairness norm |
| Unity | In-group bias |

Kahneman explains *why* the mechanism exists (evolutionary architecture of fast thinking). Cialdini describes *how* to trigger it (or defend against the trigger).

### ↔ Taleb (Skin in the Game)

[Skin in the Game](./skin-in-the-game.md) is the structural antidote to authority and social proof abuse. If an authority figure bears the consequences of their advice, the authority signal becomes genuinely informative. If not, it's noise. Taleb's insistence on accountability filters out Cialdini's most dangerous case: authority symbols without real expertise or stakes.

### ↔ Dalio (Radical Transparency / Idea Meritocracy)

[Radical Transparency](./radical-transparency.md) neutralises several Cialdini distortions:
- Authority bias → override with track record weighting ([Idea Meritocracy](./idea-meritocracy.md))
- Liking bias → radical honesty regardless of relationship
- Commitment bias → explicit norms around updating prior positions

Bridgewater's design is, in part, an institutional response to the compliance failures Cialdini documents.

### ↔ Christensen (Marginal Cost Trap / 100% Rule)

[Commitment and Consistency](./commitment-and-consistency.md) explains *mechanically* why [Christensen's 100% integrity rule](./how-will-you-measure-your-life.md) is correct. The first exception reshapes self-image ("I'm someone who makes exceptions"). The second exception is easier — you're now consistent with the prior exception. By exception five, the exceptions *are* the pattern. The Cialdini mechanism shows why 98% commitment collapses: each exception triggers consistency pressure toward the next.

### ↔ Moore (Crossing the Chasm)

[Social Proof](./social-proof.md) is the psychological engine behind [pragmatist herd behaviour](./crossing-the-chasm.md). Pragmatists (Early Majority) don't move until they see similar peers moving — a direct application of social proof. Moore's prescription (earn pragmatist references, dominate the beachhead so everyone in the segment knows your name) is a deliberate social proof construction strategy.
