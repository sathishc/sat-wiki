# Broken Window Fallacy

Introduced by [Frédéric Bastiat](../people/frederic-bastiat.md) in 1850; popularised for modern readers by [Henry Hazlitt](../people/henry-hazlitt.md). The foundational illustration of why destruction does not create net wealth — and why thinking only about visible consequences leads to systematically wrong conclusions.

## The Parable

A shopkeeper's son breaks a shop window. Bystanders console the shopkeeper:

> "Look on the bright side — the glazier will get work! The glazier will spend that money, and trade will circulate. It's an ill wind that blows nobody good!"

Bastiat's response: this reasoning is wrong, and understanding *why* it is wrong is the key to all sound economic thinking.

**What is seen:** The glazier gets paid six francs. A transaction occurs. Economic activity is visible.

**What is not seen:** The shopkeeper would have spent those six francs on new shoes. The cobbler does not get that sale. The shoes never exist. The shopkeeper ends up with a window (back to where he started) instead of a window and shoes.

**Net result:** Society has the same window it had before, but one pair of shoes that would have existed does not. The destruction has not created wealth — it has destroyed it.

## The Formal Error

The fallacy is treating a *redistribution* as a *creation*. The glazier gains six francs. The cobbler loses six francs of potential business. The shopkeeper has less real wealth. No new value has been created — value has been shuffled, with a net loss.

More precisely: every act of destruction forecloses what would have been done with those resources instead. The destroyed value is the [opportunity cost](./seen-vs-unseen.md) — and it is invisible.

## Why It Persists

The fallacy is compelling because one effect is concentrated and visible (the glazier's work) while the other is diffuse and counterfactual (the cobbler's non-sale). The glazier exists as a real person; the cobbler's forgone business is an abstraction. Humans weight concrete, visible outcomes far more than abstract counterfactuals.

Politicians, journalists, and lobbyists have exploited this asymmetry systematically for centuries.

## Modern Applications

The same logical error underlies dozens of persistent policy fallacies:

| Policy | Broken Window Version |
|---|---|
| War spending | "The war created jobs!" (yes, and destroyed the capital, labour, and resources those jobs would otherwise have built) |
| Natural disaster "stimulus" | "The hurricane will boost construction!" (yes, by replacing what was destroyed; net: worse off) |
| Subsidies | "We saved X jobs in industry Y!" (yes, by taxing away resources from industries A–Z) |
| Trade protectionism | "We kept factory jobs!" (yes, by making every consumer pay higher prices) |
| "Cash for Clunkers" | "We boosted auto sales!" (yes, by destroying working cars and taxing future buyers) |
