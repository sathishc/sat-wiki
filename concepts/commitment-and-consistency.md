# Commitment and Consistency

The second of [Cialdini's](../people/robert-cialdini.md) [seven principles of influence](./principles-of-influence.md). Once we commit to a position, belief, or behaviour — especially publicly or in writing — we feel strong pressure to remain consistent with it.

## The Mechanism

We are motivated to appear consistent because inconsistency signals unreliability — to others and to ourselves. We use our past behaviour as evidence of our own values: *"I did X, so I must be the kind of person who does X."* The commitment reshapes self-image, which then drives future behaviour.

**Commitment strength is amplified by:**
- **Writing it down** — externalises the commitment; becomes evidence about who you are
- **Public declaration** — social witnesses increase the cost of inconsistency
- **Effort invested** — the harder the commitment was to make, the more it locks in (cognitive dissonance reduction)
- **Perceived freedom** — commitments felt as freely chosen are more binding than coerced ones

## Techniques

**Foot-in-the-door:** Get a small commitment first; larger requests follow more easily. Once someone agrees to a small ask, they're now "the kind of person who agrees to this." Escalating asks leverage that identity.

**Lowball:** Agree to a deal, then change the terms after commitment is made. People often proceed anyway because they've already committed mentally — and backing out would be inconsistent.

**Written pledge cards:** Fundraisers, health campaigns, and sales all use written commitments because follow-through rates are dramatically higher than verbal commitments.

## The Identity Mechanism

This is deeper than habit. Commitments don't just create behavioural momentum — they update the self-concept. A person who signs a petition about environmental issues begins to see themselves as "an environmentalist." Future behaviour aligns with that identity even without the petition being visible.

This is why **public commitment** is so powerful: once you've declared something to others, backing out requires not just changing behaviour but publicly revising your identity.

## The Dark Side: Sunk Cost and Escalation

Commitment & Consistency is the psychological engine of the sunk cost fallacy — continuing a failing course of action because abandoning it would be inconsistent with prior commitment and investment. The rational question ("what's the best choice going forward?") is overridden by the consistency pressure ("I've already committed to this").

This also explains why organisations double down on failing strategies, why leaders won't admit mistakes, and why the [Planning Fallacy](./planning-fallacy.md) is so hard to correct mid-project.

## Cross-references

**↔ [How Will You Measure Your Life?](./how-will-you-measure-your-life.md) (Christensen — 100% integrity rule)**
Commitment & Consistency is the mechanism behind the marginal cost trap. The first ethical exception updates self-image ("I'm someone who makes exceptions under pressure"). The next exception is now *consistent* with that updated identity. By exception five, the exceptions are the pattern. This is why Christensen's 100% rule is correct: the first exception is not just a slip — it's an identity update that makes subsequent slips easier.

**↔ [Cognitive Biases (Kahneman)](./cognitive-biases-kahneman.md)**
Anchoring is the numerical version of commitment — the first number heard shapes all subsequent judgments. WYSIATI is the epistemic version: once a coherent story is formed, new information is filtered through it. Both are consistency mechanisms applied to belief rather than action.

**↔ [Radical Transparency](./radical-transparency.md) (Dalio)**
Dalio's explicit norm of *updating* beliefs based on evidence is a designed antidote to consistency bias. Bridgewater trains people to separate "I was wrong" from "I am wrong" — acknowledging a bad prior position without it threatening identity.

## Defence

Ask: *"If I hadn't already committed, would I make this decision today?"* Separate the original commitment from the current choice. You are not obligated to remain consistent with a past self who had less information. Changing your mind in light of new evidence is not inconsistency — it's rationality.
