# The Power Law

## One-Line Definition

In venture capital and startup outcomes, returns follow a **power law** — a tiny number of investments return more than all the others combined. The same logic applies to individual choices and careers.

## The VC Reality

A typical VC fund:
- Makes 20–30 investments
- Expects most to return nothing or little
- Expects **one investment to return more than the entire fund**
- May get one or two more that return 5–10×

This is not a bell curve. It's not "some do well, some do badly." It's an extreme distribution where the best outcome dominates everything else.

*Example:* Founders Fund's investment in Facebook returned more than all other investments combined. This is normal for top-tier VC.

## Implications for VCs

- You **cannot afford to miss the best deals**. Missing the one big winner while avoiding bad investments is not a good strategy.
- Every investment must have *potential* to be the fund-returner. If a deal clearly can't be a 100× outcome, it probably shouldn't be made.
- Diversification logic (spread risk across many bets) breaks down under power law — concentration in the best opportunities dominates.

## Implications for Founders

The power law applies to your **own company's strategy too**:
- One distribution channel will likely outperform all others combined
- One product line will likely generate most of the revenue
- One key hire may matter more than all other hires

*"Most businesses never need to understand power laws, but [startup] founders always do."* — Thiel

## Implications for Individuals

Thiel extends this to career decisions:
- It's better to **own a small piece of one exceptional thing** than a large piece of something mediocre
- The difference between the best job and the second-best job is greater than the difference between the second-best and the worst
- Diversifying your career (hedge your bets, keep options open) is the wrong model for building exceptional outcomes
