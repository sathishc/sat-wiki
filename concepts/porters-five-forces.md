# Porter's Five Forces

Introduced by [Michael Porter](../people/michael-porter.md) in *Competitive Strategy* (1980). The framework for analysing the competitive structure of an industry and assessing its average long-run profit potential. The five forces are the competitive dynamics that collectively determine how much value an industry can create — and how much of that value firms in the industry can keep.

> "The five competitive forces reveal that competition in an industry goes well beyond the established players. Customers, suppliers, substitutes, and potential entrants are all 'competitors' to firms in the industry." — Porter

## The Five Forces

### 1. Threat of New Entrants

New entrants bring new capacity and desire for market share, which puts pressure on prices and margins. The severity of this threat depends on **barriers to entry**:

- **Economies of scale** — incumbents have cost advantages from volume; entrants must enter at large scale (risky) or accept a cost disadvantage
- **Brand identity and switching costs** — buyers are loyal or face costs of switching; the entrant must invest heavily to overcome this
- **Capital requirements** — some industries require massive upfront investment (semiconductor fabs, airline fleets); this deters casual entry
- **Access to distribution channels** — if incumbents have locked up shelf space, dealer networks, or platform access, entrants struggle to reach customers
- **Proprietary technology or cost advantages** — know-how, patents, learning curve effects, access to raw materials
- **Government policy** — licensing, regulation, environmental rules can limit or foreclose entry

**Strategic implication**: High barriers to entry = more attractive industry (existing firms can earn above-average returns). Low barriers = constant new entry keeps returns thin.

### 2. Bargaining Power of Suppliers

Suppliers can capture value from the industry by raising prices or reducing quality. Supplier power is high when:
- The supplier group is **concentrated** (few suppliers serving many buyers)
- There are **no substitutes** for the supplier's input
- The input is **critical** to the buyer's product quality
- **Switching costs** are high
- Suppliers can credibly **threaten forward integration** (supplying directly to end customers)
- The buyer is not important to the supplier (small volume relative to supplier's total sales)

**Classic example**: Intel's bargaining power over PC makers in the 1990s — the "Intel Inside" campaign both created buyer preference and made switching processors costly for OEMs.

### 3. Bargaining Power of Buyers

Buyers can capture value by forcing prices down, demanding higher quality, or playing competitors against each other. Buyer power is high when:
- Buyers are **concentrated** or purchase in large volumes
- The product is **undifferentiated** (buyers can easily switch)
- Buyers face **low switching costs**
- Buyers have **full information** (prices, costs, demand)
- Buyers can credibly **threaten backward integration** (making the product themselves)
- The product represents a **significant fraction of buyer's cost** (incentivises buyers to shop hard)

**Insight**: Porter distinguishes between price sensitivity (how much buyers care about price) and bargaining leverage (how much power they have to extract concessions). Both matter but are independent.

### 4. Threat of Substitute Products or Services

Substitutes perform the same function as the industry's product but via a different means. They limit an industry's price ceiling — if prices rise above the substitute's threshold, buyers switch. The threat is high when:
- The substitute offers an **attractive price-performance trade-off**
- Switching to the substitute is low-cost for buyers
- The substitute is produced by high-profit industries that can subsidise price competition

**Examples**: videoconferencing as a substitute for business air travel; email as a substitute for letter post; streaming as a substitute for cinema.

### 5. Rivalry Among Existing Competitors

Competitive rivalry directly shapes industry profitability through price competition, advertising battles, product introductions, and service improvements. Rivalry is intense when:
- **Many competitors** of similar size and capability
- **Slow industry growth** — firms must fight for share to grow
- **High fixed or storage costs** — incentive to cut price to fill capacity
- **Low differentiation and low switching costs** — buyers switch on price alone
- **High strategic stakes** — competitors are committed to the industry for strategic reasons beyond its economics
- **High exit barriers** — specialised assets, emotional commitment, strategic interrelationships make it costly to leave even when returns are poor

**The exit barrier / entry barrier matrix**: High entry barriers + low exit barriers = best returns. High entry barriers + high exit barriers = high but volatile returns (firms won't leave even when times are bad). Low entry barriers + low exit barriers = low but stable returns. Low entry barriers + high exit barriers = worst outcome.

## How to Use the Framework

### Step 1: Assess industry attractiveness

Rate each force (low / moderate / high threat). The collective strength of the five forces determines the **average profitability potential** of the industry. This is about the industry, not your firm's position — a highly attractive industry raises all boats.

**High-profitability industries** (all forces weak): pharmaceutical drugs (strong IP, high switching costs, fragmented buyers, few substitutes, high entry barriers).

**Low-profitability industries** (multiple forces strong): commodity chemicals, airline industry (powerful buyers + substitutes + intense rivalry + low switching costs).

### Step 2: Identify where power sits

Which forces are dominant? This tells you where strategic attention should focus. If buyer power is the dominant force, differentiation and switching-cost strategies matter most. If supplier power dominates, backward integration or supplier diversification becomes strategic.

### Step 3: Identify strategic options

Can you **shift** the forces? Can you:
- Build barriers to entry (invest in brand, switch-cost lock-in, scale)?
- Reduce buyer power (differentiate so price isn't the issue)?
- Reduce supplier power (backward integrate, develop alternate suppliers)?
- Reduce substitute threat (improve price-performance relative to substitute)?
- Reduce rivalry (differentiate to avoid head-to-head price competition; focus on a segment with fewer rivals)?

### Step 4: Anticipate force evolution

Industries are not static. How will each force change? Entry barriers erode as technology diffuses. Buyer power increases as buyers become more sophisticated. Substitutes improve over time. Anticipating structural change before rivals do creates advantage.

## Limitations and Critiques

### 1. Static framework (Christensen)

Porter's framework captures an industry at a moment in time. [Christensen's disruption mechanism](./innovators-dilemma.md) shows that disruptors don't just enter the industry — they change the performance trajectory, redefine what "good" means, and eventually destroy the industry structure itself. A perfectly accurate Five Forces analysis in 1995 would not have predicted Napster, Netflix, or smartphones.

The disruption mechanism is what happens when "threat of new entrants" is rated low (entrants are bad at what incumbents do) but the entrants are improving rapidly along a different dimension incumbents aren't tracking.

### 2. Ignores resources and capabilities (Prahalad / RBV)

[C.K. Prahalad](../people/ck-prahalad.md) and Gary Hamel, along with Jay Barney's resource-based view, argued that Porter's outside-in framework misses the most important source of competitive advantage: internal capabilities that are hard to imitate. Positioning is imitable; deep [core competency](./core-competency.md) is not. Two firms in the same industry, facing the same five forces, can earn radically different returns if one has rare capabilities the other lacks.

### 3. Blue Ocean critique (Kim / Mauborgne)

*Blue Ocean Strategy* (2005) argued that the best strategy is not to compete within existing industry structure at all — it is to reconstruct industry boundaries to make competition irrelevant. The five forces assume a fixed competitive space; Blue Ocean strategy creates uncontested market space. This is related to Prahalad's "competing for the future" logic: the winner shapes the industry, not just positions within it.

### 4. Network effects and platforms

In platform and ecosystem businesses (Apple App Store, Amazon Marketplace, Google Search), the five forces framework is less useful because:
- The unit of competition is the **ecosystem**, not the industry
- Network effects create winner-take-all dynamics that don't fit neatly into the framework
- Entry barriers are created by network effects, not just scale or IP

### 5. Ignores firm-specific execution

Even within an industry with weak structural forces, exceptional execution, culture, and learning can create sustained outperformance. Porter's framework is about average industry returns, not about why specific firms outperform. The [value chain](./value-chain.md) is Porter's answer to this — but critics argue it underweights the organisational and cultural dimensions.
