# How Will You Measure Your Life?

[Clayton Christensen's](../people/clayton-christensen.md) most personal work (2012, co-authored with James Allworth and Karen Dillon). Adapted from a Harvard Business School commencement speech. The book applies the same rigorous frameworks Christensen used to analyse businesses — disruption, marginal cost, resource allocation, jobs to be done — to the question of how to build a meaningful life.

> "Don't worry about the level of individual prominence you have achieved; worry about the individuals you have helped become better people."

## Origin

Christensen watched brilliant HBS classmates become professionally successful and personally wrecked — divorced, estranged from their children, some in prison. Jeffrey Skilling of Enron was a classmate. The pattern troubled him enough to build a course around it.

His diagnosis: the same forces that cause good companies to fail (wrong metrics, marginal cost thinking, misaligned incentives) cause good people to fail — at being happy, being present, being honest.

## The Three Questions

1. **How can I be sure I'll be happy in my career?**
2. **How can I be sure my relationships with family and close friends become an enduring source of happiness?**
3. **How can I stay out of jail?** (About integrity — not a joke)

## Question 1: Career & Motivation

### Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory

Christensen builds on Frederick Herzberg's research:

| Type | Examples | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Hygiene factors** | Salary, status, security, working conditions | Absence → dissatisfaction. Presence → neutral. Does NOT motivate. |
| **Motivators** | Challenge, growth, meaning, autonomy, recognition for achievement | Presence → genuine engagement and satisfaction |

The trap: people optimise for hygiene factors (chasing salary, prestige, title) and wonder why they feel empty. Hygiene factors remove pain; they don't create joy.

Real career satisfaction comes from intrinsic motivators — and these are mostly non-negotiable. No amount of salary compensates for work that is unchallenging, meaningless, or autonomy-free.

### Deliberate vs. Emergent Strategy

Early in a career, treat strategy as a portfolio of hypotheses:
- Have deliberate plans, but hold them loosely
- Be open to unexpected opportunities that *feel right* — these are data
- Don't over-optimise for the plan at the expense of real signals

Over time, the career strategy should be *discovered*, not just executed. The plan is a starting hypothesis; experience is the test.

## Question 2: Relationships & Family

### The Marginal Cost Trap in Relationships

The mechanism that destroys careers and families is identical:

> "The marginal cost of doing something wrong 'just this once' always seems low."

Skipping dinner to finish a deal: the marginal cost tonight is low. But the pattern compounds. After ten years of "just this once," the relationship is gone. The cost was never evaluated in full — only at the margin.

Companies make this mistake too: "just this one customer exception," "just this one strategic detour." The marginal cost always looks low. The full cost only becomes visible later.

### Jobs to Be Done in Relationships

What job is your spouse *actually* hiring you to do? It may not be what you think. It is rarely "earn more money." It is often something like: "be genuinely present," "make me feel understood," "be someone I can rely on."

The JTBD lens applied here: observe what the people you love are actually trying to accomplish, emotionally and socially — and design your behaviour to do that job well.

### The School of Experience

Children need challenges, setbacks, and the chance to fail. Overprotection — "helicoptering" — feels loving but robs children of the experiences that build genuine capability.

This is the RPM lens applied to parenting: capabilities live in *experiences*, not in resources. Giving a child every advantage (resources) while shielding them from adversity means they never develop the processes and values that make them capable adults.

Hire managers for the right experiences, not just credentials. Raise children the same way.

## Question 3: Integrity — The 100% Rule

### Marginal Cost and Ethics

> "The marginal cost of doing something wrong 'just this once' always seems low. It succinctly explains how good people with strong values can slip into bad behaviour and ruin their lives and careers."

Integrity cannot be maintained at 98%. The 2% exception — the "just this once" — is the mechanism of erosion. Each exception makes the next one easier, lowers the threshold, and narrows the perceived distance between you and people you never imagined becoming.

### 100% Commitment Is Easier Than 98%

This is Christensen's most counterintuitive claim, and also the most important:

It is cognitively and morally *easier* to commit 100% to a principle than to commit 98% and manage exceptions case by case.

At 100%, there is no decision to make. At 98%, every situation becomes a negotiation — and in high-pressure moments, with motivated reasoning available, the negotiation tilts toward the exception.

The practical implication: decide in advance, in calm conditions, that certain lines will never be crossed. Not "probably not" — never. This eliminates the decision space where integrity erodes.

### Full Cost vs. Marginal Cost

Always evaluate the *full cost* of a decision — not just the incremental cost. The full cost of "just this once" includes:
- The identity you are becoming
- The trust you are destroying
- The precedent you are setting for yourself
- The pattern you are normalising

The full cost is almost always much higher than the marginal cost. The marginal cost frame is a lie your motivated reasoning tells you.

## The Metric

The concluding argument. What will you measure your life by?

Revenue: No.
Awards: No.
Status: No.

> "The metric is the individual lives you have touched — the depth of your relationships, the people you helped become better people."

The deepest act of love, in Christensen's view, is not giving resources but helping someone develop their own potential. This applies to children, employees, students, and everyone you have influence over.

Choose your metric before you're on your deathbed trying to retrofit one.
