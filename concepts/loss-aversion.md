# Loss Aversion and Prospect Theory

Two of [Kahneman](../people/daniel-kahneman.md) and Tversky's most important contributions. Loss aversion is the empirical observation; Prospect Theory is the formal model that explains it. Both replace the classical economics assumption of rational expected utility with something that actually matches observed human behaviour.

## Loss Aversion

**The finding:** Losses loom larger than gains of equivalent size. The pain of losing £100 is roughly twice as intense as the pleasure of gaining £100.

> "For most people, the fear of losing $100 is more intense than the hope of gaining $150."

This is not irrational in an evolutionary sense — organisms that weight losses heavily survive better. But it produces systematic, predictable distortions in decision-making.

**Consequences:**

| Behaviour | Root in Loss Aversion |
|---|---|
| Holding losing investments too long | Selling = making the loss real; not selling = the loss is still "unrealised" |
| Refusing good gambles | A 50/50 bet for £100 gain vs £100 loss feels unattractive because the loss hurts ~2x |
| Status quo bias | Change risks loss; staying put risks nothing visible |
| Negotiation anchoring | The first number sets the reference point; moving away from it feels like loss |
| Risk aversion in gains, risk seeking in losses | People accept sure gains over gambles; people gamble to avoid sure losses |

## Prospect Theory

The formal model (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979). Three key features:

**1. Reference dependence**
People evaluate outcomes relative to a reference point (usually the status quo), not in absolute terms. Gaining £500 when you expected £0 feels different from gaining £500 when you expected £1,000 — even though the absolute outcome is the same.

**2. Loss aversion**
The value function is steeper for losses than for gains. The slope is approximately 2:1 — a loss of X is felt as about twice as painful as a gain of X is felt as pleasurable.

**3. Diminishing sensitivity**
Moving from £0 to £100 feels more significant than moving from £900 to £1,000. The value function is concave in gains and convex in losses — the marginal impact diminishes as you move further from the reference point.

**4. Probability weighting**
People overweight small probabilities (lotteries, insurance) and underweight large but not certain probabilities. A 1% chance of winning feels more than 1/100th as good as a certain win. A 99% chance of winning feels worse than a certain win by more than 1%.

## Implications for Decision-Making

**Framing effects:** How a choice is presented changes what people choose — even when the underlying math is identical. "90% survival rate" and "10% mortality rate" produce different choices. This is a direct implication of prospect theory's reference dependence.

**Endowment effect:** People value things they own more than identical things they don't own. Ownership sets the reference point; selling feels like loss.

**Sunk cost fallacy:** Past losses shift the reference point. People irrationally continue failing projects because stopping means "realising" the sunk cost as a final loss.

## Cross-References

**[Taleb](../people/nassim-taleb.md)'s [Barbell Strategy](./barbell-strategy.md):**
The barbell is partly a loss-aversion hack. By capping downside at the safe end, you remove the psychological agony of large losses, freeing you to take calculated risks at the speculative end. Structurally, you're replacing a loss-averse gamble with a position where you can't lose catastrophically.

**[Reinertsen](../people/donald-reinertsen.md)'s [Cost of Delay](./cost-of-delay.md):**
Loss aversion distorts prioritisation. Teams overweight the certain cost of spending engineering time (a real loss) and underweight the diffuse cost of delayed delivery (an abstract loss). CoD makes the abstract loss concrete and comparable.

**[Dalio](../people/ray-dalio.md)'s "Are Right, A Lot":**
Loss aversion makes people cling to wrong positions — changing your mind means admitting a mistake (a loss of status). Dalio's [radical transparency](./radical-transparency.md) is partly a cultural norm designed to de-weaponise this: being wrong is expected and fine if you update.

**[Amazon LP #13](./amazon-leadership-principles.md) "Disagree and Commit":**
Loss aversion makes people avoid voicing disagreement (risks social loss). The principle forces the uncomfortable action up front — ensuring System 2 reasoning isn't suppressed by loss-avoidant System 1 comfort-seeking.
