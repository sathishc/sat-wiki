# Givers, Takers, and Matchers

Adam Grant's framework from *Give and Take* (2013): the three fundamental **reciprocity styles** that shape how people interact professionally and what their long-run outcomes look like.

---

## The Three Styles

### Takers
Takers believe the world is competitive and zero-sum. They take more than they give — in conversations, in recognition, in credit. Self-promotion is habitual; networking is transactional; others are instruments. Takers aren't necessarily malicious; many genuinely believe that looking out for themselves is how success works.

**Organisational signature:** hoarding information, claiming credit for group work, talking more than listening, cultivating upward relationships while neglecting peers and subordinates.

### Matchers
Matchers operate on balance: you scratch my back, I'll scratch yours. They track favours carefully and ensure exchanges are fair. Matchers are the plurality of most workplaces. They are reliable partners but will not extend generosity beyond what they expect to receive — which limits the scope of help they give and get.

**Organisational signature:** conditional helpfulness ("I'll help you if you've helped me or might help me"); strong sense of fairness; uncomfortable with imbalance in either direction.

### Givers
Givers contribute without keeping score. They help because it's valuable to the recipient, not because they expect a return. Givers are genuinely interested in others' success and will invest time, expertise, and connections without calculating the ROI.

**The critical distinction:**
- **Selfless givers** give indiscriminately, including to takers who exploit them. They end up at the **bottom** of success ladders — burned out, time-poor, resentful.
- **Otherish givers** are genuinely other-focused but also set limits: they give in ways that are sustainable, they are more cautious with clear takers, and they attend to their own interests alongside others'. They end up at the **top**.

> "Being otherish means being willing to give more than you receive, but still keeping your own interests in sight." — Adam Grant

---

## The Counterintuitive Finding

**Givers are simultaneously at the bottom AND the top of every success metric Grant studied.**

This resolves the apparent contradiction: it's not that givers win or lose — it's that *how* they give determines which outcome they get. The differentiator is not selflessness vs. selfishness but selflessness vs. *otherish*.

Takers and matchers cluster in the middle.

---

## Why Givers Build the Best Networks

### Dormant Ties
Givers help people without keeping score — which means they accumulate a wide, diverse network of people who remember them positively. *Dormant ties* — connections that have gone quiet for years — are often the highest-value network nodes. They've moved in different circles and have access to information and opportunities that are genuinely new to you. Givers have more dormant ties because they helped people when there was no obvious return.

### Trust and Reputation
In most organisations, your reputation precedes you. Givers develop a reputation as someone worth knowing — not because they're performing generosity, but because it shows up repeatedly. This reputation compounds: people bring givers opportunities, introductions, and information precisely because they know it will be used well.

---

## Why Givers Are More Creative

Perspective-taking — the mental act of inhabiting someone else's viewpoint to understand what they need — is the core cognitive practice of giving. It turns out this same mental habit is also one of the foundations of creative thinking: expanding the solution space by viewing a problem from multiple angles.

Givers practise perspective-taking constantly. As a result, they tend to generate more diverse, novel solutions than takers (who are focused on their own position) or matchers (who are focused on balance).

---

## Why Organisations Full of Takers Are Fragile

When a culture tips toward takers:

1. **Information hoarding** — sharing knowledge means giving away an advantage; people hoard what they know
2. **Collaboration collapse** — every interaction becomes zero-sum; true teamwork disappears
3. **Trust deficit** — every generous act is interpreted as strategic; authentic help becomes invisible
4. **Star-system distortion** — self-promoters are rewarded for visibility, not output; genuine contributors are under-recognised
5. **Talent flight** — givers and matchers eventually recognise the environment and leave

The result is an organisation that looks like it has strong individuals but systematically underperforms on any task requiring genuine cooperation.

---

## Practical Implications

- **The 5-Minute Favour:** Give in ways that cost you little but are highly valuable to the recipient. Introductions, quick feedback, sharing relevant information. Sustainable giving has a high value-to-cost ratio.
- **Screen for reciprocity style early.** Takers often reveal themselves through small signals: how they talk about others, whether they ask about you or only about themselves, how they respond when you can't help them.
- **Otherish giving is a long-term strategy.** The returns are real but slow. The networks compound, the reputation builds, the trust deepens — over years, not weeks.
