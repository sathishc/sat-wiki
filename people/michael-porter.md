# Michael Porter

Michael E. Porter (born 1947). Bishop William Lawrence University Professor at Harvard Business School — the highest honour the university confers. The most-cited scholar in economics and business combined. His two landmark works, *Competitive Strategy* (1980) and *Competitive Advantage* (1985), transformed strategic management from a field of CEO intuition and war metaphors into a rigorous analytical discipline grounded in industrial organisation economics.

> "If you don't have a competitive advantage, don't compete."

> "Strategy is the creation of a unique and valuable position, involving a different set of activities." (*What Is Strategy?*, HBR 1996)

## Biography

- Born 1947. BSE in aerospace and mechanical engineering, Princeton, 1969. MBA, Harvard Business School, 1971. PhD in Business Economics (industrial organisation), Harvard, 1973.
- Joined HBS faculty 1973. By age 26 he was working on the book that would become *Competitive Strategy*.
- Published *Competitive Strategy* in 1980 — winner of the McKinsey Award. Published *Competitive Advantage* in 1985. *The Competitive Advantage of Nations* in 1990.
- 1996 HBR article *What Is Strategy?* clarified and defended his framework against emergent strategy critics (Mintzberg) and capability theorists (Prahalad/Hamel).
- Extended into healthcare strategy, urban clusters, social responsibility, and "shared value" (with Mark Kramer).
- Member of the Board of Governors of the American Stock Exchange; advisor to companies, governments, and international development bodies.

## Core Contributions

### The Outside-In View of Strategy

Porter's most fundamental claim: **start with the industry, then position**. Industry structure — the configuration of competitive forces — is the primary determinant of average profitability. A firm that understands its industry can find a position where it earns above-average returns by:
1. Defending itself against competitive forces
2. Influencing competitive forces in its favour
3. Anticipating shifts in competitive forces before rivals do

This is the **outside-in** view: look outside the firm (at the industry) first, then decide what to do inside.

### The Five Forces

See [Porter's Five Forces](../concepts/porters-five-forces.md) — the framework for analysing industry attractiveness and competitive dynamics. The five forces are: threat of new entrants, bargaining power of suppliers, bargaining power of buyers, threat of substitutes, and rivalry among existing competitors.

### Generic Strategies

See [Generic Strategies](../concepts/generic-strategies.md) — cost leadership, differentiation, and focus. Porter's famous warning: firms that fail to choose a clear strategy are "stuck in the middle" and earn below-average returns.

### The Value Chain

See [Value Chain](../concepts/value-chain.md) — the disaggregation of the firm into primary and support activities. Competitive advantage comes from performing these activities more cheaply or in ways that create greater buyer value.

### Competitive Advantage

See [Competitive Advantage](../concepts/competitive-advantage.md) — Porter's definition: sustainable superior performance through either cost leadership or differentiation. Competitive advantage must be grounded in the value chain and defended through barriers to imitation.

## Contrast with Other Thinkers in the Wiki

| Dimension | Porter | Prahalad | Christensen | Bezos |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Starting point** | Industry structure (outside-in) | Core competencies (inside-out) | Disruptive mechanism | Customer obsession |
| **Unit of analysis** | Industry / competitive position | [Core Competency](../concepts/core-competency.md) / market space | Value network / disruption trajectory | Flywheel / customer lifetime value |
| **Source of advantage** | Defensible position (cost or differentiation) | Hard-to-imitate bundle of skills | Being before the disruption | Reinforcing loop at scale |
| **Strategic advice to incumbents** | Understand forces; choose a position; build fit | Compete for the future; don't just defend today | Create separate disruption units; watch the trajectory | Stay Day 1; never stop inventing |
| **View of competition** | Structural and industry-level | Competency-based reshaping of industries | Disruption from below or fringe | Competition is irrelevant; focus on the customer |
| **Time horizon** | Medium-term position | Long-term market space | Disruption arc (typically years to decades) | Long-term (Bezos thinks in decades) |
| **Weakness** | Static; ignores disruption from outside industry structure; over-emphasises positioning over capabilities | Can underplay structural forces and industry dynamics | Less prescriptive about how incumbents should actually compete today | Context-specific to platform/network businesses |

**Key tension with [Prahalad](./ck-prahalad.md):** Porter says look outside first — understand the industry, then position. Prahalad says look inside first — understand what you are uniquely capable of, then figure out which markets to create. This outside-in vs. inside-out debate is one of the defining debates in strategic management. Neither is simply right; they are complementary lenses. Porter is better at explaining industry-level dynamics; Prahalad is better at explaining why some firms escape those dynamics by building unique capabilities.

**Key tension with [Christensen](./clayton-christensen.md):** Porter's framework assumes industry structure is relatively stable. Christensen showed that disruptors don't just reposition within an existing structure — they destroy the structure itself by changing the performance trajectory. A perfect Porter analysis in 1990 would not have predicted Netflix destroying Blockbuster or Amazon destroying retail. Christensen's mechanism is what happens when Porter's "threat of new entrants" barrier fails.

**Convergence with [Bezos](./jeff-bezos.md):** Amazon's strategy is interesting precisely because it violates Porter's "stuck in the middle" warning at the surface level — it pursues cost leadership AND differentiation simultaneously. Porter would say this is possible only through rare strategic fit and reinforcing loops (which is exactly what the Amazon flywheel is). Porter's value chain framework is a useful lens for understanding where Amazon earns its margin (AWS, advertising, Marketplace) vs. where it invests (logistics, fulfilment).

## Key Quotes

> "The essence of strategy formulation is coping with competition."

> "Competitive advantage cannot be understood by looking at a firm as a whole. It stems from the many discrete activities a firm performs in designing, producing, marketing, delivering, and supporting its product."

> "Competitive advantage grows out of value a firm is able to create for its buyers that exceeds the firm's cost of creating it."

> "Strategy is about making choices, trade-offs; it's about deliberately choosing to be different."

> "The company without a strategy is willing to try anything."

> "Operational effectiveness is not strategy."

## Legacy

Porter's frameworks dominated corporate strategy from 1980 to the mid-1990s. The challenge from resource-based view theorists (Barney, Prahalad/Hamel) shifted attention from industry structure to internal capabilities. The rise of platform economics, network effects, and technology disruption created further challenges for static positioning frameworks.

Nevertheless:
- Five Forces remains the standard first-pass tool for industry analysis in every business school
- The value chain is the standard tool for operations and margin analysis
- Generic strategies are the starting vocabulary of competitive positioning
- *What Is Strategy?* (1996) remains one of the most assigned HBR articles of all time
- His diagnostic tools have been extended to healthcare, nations, clusters, and social enterprise

Porter's enduring contribution is methodological: he showed that strategy can be analysed rigorously, not just intuited. Whatever its limitations, that shift in how we think about competition is permanent.
