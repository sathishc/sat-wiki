# Henry Hazlitt

American journalist and economic writer (1894–1993). Chief editorial writer at the *New York Times*, weekly columnist at *Newsweek*, founding vice-president of the Foundation for Economic Education. Author of *Economics in One Lesson* (1946) — one of the best-selling economics books of all time, with over a million copies sold.

> "The art of economics consists in looking not merely at the immediate but at the longer effects of any act or policy; it consists in tracing the consequences of that policy not merely for one group but for all groups."

Hazlitt was not an academic economist. He was a journalist who could translate difficult ideas into clear prose. His gift was making the invisible consequences of policy visible to ordinary readers.

## Intellectual Lineage

Hazlitt stood at the intersection of three traditions:

- **[Frédéric Bastiat](./frederic-bastiat.md)** — French economist (1801–1850) whose essay *"That Which Is Seen and That Which Is Not Seen"* (1850) is the direct ancestor of the entire book. Hazlitt explicitly acknowledges this.
- **Ludwig von Mises** — Austrian economist; Hazlitt was deeply influenced by Mises's framework of praxeology and free-market economics
- **Friedrich Hayek** — Austrian School; epistemic humility about central planning

## The One Lesson

The entire book reduces to a single principle:

> "The art of economics consists in looking not merely at the immediate but at the longer effects of any act or policy; it consists in tracing the consequences of that policy not merely for one group but for all groups."

Every chapter in the book applies this lens to a different economic fallacy — demonstrating that each fallacy fails precisely because it only looks at the immediate, visible effect on one group, and ignores the longer-term, invisible effects on all others.

## The 24 Fallacies Examined

Hazlitt applied the one lesson to demolish a series of then-common (and still-common) economic beliefs:

| Fallacy | What It Gets Wrong |
|---|---|
| [Broken Window Fallacy](../concepts/broken-window-fallacy.md) | Destruction doesn't create net wealth — it destroys it |
| Public works create employment | They redirect resources from private use; the unseen jobs lost equal the seen jobs created |
| Tariffs protect industries | They protect one industry by taxing every consumer and every other industry |
| Price controls help consumers | They cause shortages and black markets; seen: lower prices; unseen: missing supply |
| Minimum wage laws help workers | They price out the least productive workers; seen: higher wages; unseen: unemployment |
| Inflation stimulates the economy | It redistributes wealth from creditors to debtors; destroys the price system |
| Saving hurts the economy | Saving funds investment; "paradox of thrift" is false at the macro level |
| Machines destroy jobs | They free labour for other uses; productivity gain is universally distributed over time |
| Rent control helps tenants | It creates housing shortages and destroys maintenance incentives |

## The Seen vs The Unseen — The Master Framework

The entire book is a long elaboration of Bastiat's insight: **economic fallacies occur because we see the immediate, concentrated effect and miss the diffuse, delayed effect.**

- A tariff creates **visible** jobs in the protected industry
- It destroys **invisible** jobs in every industry that must now pay higher prices for steel/aluminium/etc.

- A stimulus programme creates **visible** construction jobs
- It destroys **invisible** jobs in the private sector that the taxes or borrowing suppressed

- Rent controls create **visible** affordable rent for current tenants
- They create **invisible** housing shortages, reduced maintenance, and discouraged construction

The asymmetry is systematic: benefits are concentrated and immediate; costs are diffuse and delayed. Politicians exploit this systematically.

## Contrast with Other Thinkers in the Wiki

| Dimension | Hazlitt/Bastiat | Taleb | Dalio | Reinertsen |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Primary lens** | Seen vs unseen consequences | Visible vs hidden risk | Explicit principles | Visible vs hidden queues |
| **On intervention** | Sceptical; unintended consequences dominate | Sceptical; naive intervention creates fragility | Contextual; principles govern | Sceptical; over-control creates queues |
| **On time horizon** | Long-run effects override short-run | Long-run antifragility | Compound principles over decades | [Cost of Delay](../concepts/cost-of-delay.md) over time |
| **Core method** | Trace all consequences, all groups | Build for unknown unknowns | Systematise and back-test | Quantify economic impact |

**Key convergence:** Hazlitt, Taleb, and Reinertsen all make the same structural argument from different domains: **the unseen/unmeasured consequences dominate**. The broken window fallacy, hidden [queues](../concepts/queues-in-product-development.md), and [Black Swans](../concepts/black-swan.md) are all failures of the same cognitive error — optimising for what is visible while ignoring what is not.

## Key Quotes

> "In the long run, the 'temporary' always becomes permanent and the 'emergency' never ends."

> "The real question is not what can be seen but what cannot be seen — the jobs that were not created, the goods that were not made, the services that were not rendered."

> "Every time we try to lift a stone we cannot see, we drop a stone we can see."
