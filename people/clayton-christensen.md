# Clayton Christensen

American academic, business consultant, and author (1952–2020). Kim B. Clark Professor at Harvard Business School. Widely regarded as one of the most influential management thinkers of the 20th and 21st centuries — named the world's most influential business thinker by *Thinkers50* multiple times.

A deeply religious man (devout Mormon), he was as concerned with *why* as with *what*. His work consistently probed the mechanisms behind success and failure, not just the patterns. He received multiple terminal diagnoses in his later years (cancer, heart attack, stroke) and continued teaching and writing until near his death.

> "The most powerful statement of love is helping another person develop their own potential."

## Core Contribution

Christensen gave the world the vocabulary and mechanics of **disruptive innovation** — explaining why good companies following good management principles still fail. His frameworks are used across strategy, venture capital, product management, and personal decision-making.

His essential insight: **good intentions + wrong theory = bad outcomes**. Whether it's a company listening too hard to its best customers, or a parent too busy to attend their child's events — the mechanism is the same. Incentives, marginal cost thinking, and misaligned metrics lead smart, well-meaning people astray.

## The Innovator's Dilemma (1997)

Christensen's core thesis: **successful companies fail precisely because they are well managed**. They listen to their best customers, invest in sustaining innovation, and rationally ignore low-end or fringe markets that don't fit their margins.

Meanwhile, disruptors enter from below — offering cheaper, simpler, "good enough" products to non-consumers or overserved customers. Incumbents dismiss them. Then disruptors improve rapidly and eat the market from underneath.

See: [Disruptive Innovation](../concepts/innovators-dilemma.md) for full mechanics.

---

## The Innovator's Solution (2003)

The prescription to the Dilemma. Key frameworks:

- **[Jobs to Be Done](../concepts/jobs-to-be-done.md):** Customers don't buy products — they *hire* them to do a job. Understanding the job (functional, emotional, social dimensions) beats demographic segmentation.
- **[RPM Framework](../concepts/innovators-solution.md):** Resources, Processes, Values — the three layers that determine what an organisation can and cannot do. Disruption fails inside incumbents when processes/values are incompatible.
- **Integration vs. modularisation:** Integrate when performance isn't yet good enough; modularise when it overshoots. This determines where profit pools sit.
- **Separate disruptive units:** Don't embed new disruptive businesses inside the parent — the incumbent's values will starve them.

## How Will You Measure Your Life? (2012)

Christensen watched brilliant HBS classmates (Jeffrey Skilling of Enron was one) become professionally successful and personally wrecked. This book applies his business frameworks to life.

### Career — Herzberg's Two Factors

- **Hygiene factors** (salary, status, security): absence causes dissatisfaction; presence does NOT create motivation
- **Motivators** (challenge, growth, meaning, autonomy): these actually drive engagement

The trap: people optimise for hygiene factors and wonder why they feel empty.

### The Marginal Cost Trap

> "The marginal cost of doing something wrong 'just this once' always seems low. It succinctly explains how good people with strong values can slip into bad behaviour and ruin their lives and careers."

**100% commitment is easier than 98% commitment.** The 2% exception is where integrity erodes. The same mechanism that causes strategy drift in companies — "just this one customer exception" — causes character drift in people.

### The Metric

On your deathbed, you won't count revenue or accolades. The metric is the *individual lives you've touched*.

## Contrast with Other Thinkers

| Dimension | Christensen | [Prahalad](./ck-prahalad.md) | [Dalio](./ray-dalio.md) | [Bezos](./jeff-bezos.md) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **On disruption** | Comes from below; incumbents can't respond rationally | Compete for future markets proactively | Build principles to avoid institutional blindness | Long-term orientation and Day 1 mentality as defence |
| **On customers** | Listen to current customers = existential risk | Current customers constrain; compete for *future* customers | Radical truth-seeking about what customers actually want | Customer obsession as durable advantage |
| **On organisation** | RPM constrains — processes/values resist disruption | Core competencies can become core rigidities | Idea meritocracy breaks internal politics | Two-pizza teams; single-threaded ownership |
| **On integrity** | Marginal cost trap; 100% commitment | — | Radical transparency; don't tolerate ethical lapses | Leadership Principle: Earn Trust |

**Key convergence with [Prahalad](./ck-prahalad.md):** Both observe that listening to current customers causes strategic blindness. Prahalad calls it "competing for the future" vs. defending today's position; Christensen calls it the disruptive innovation trap. Different vocabularies, same mechanism.

**Key convergence with [Dalio](./ray-dalio.md):** Dalio's RPM-like decomposition of organisations (resources, culture, process) mirrors Christensen's framework. Both argue that *culture and process* — not just strategy — determine what an organisation can actually do.

**Key convergence with [Kahneman](./daniel-kahneman.md):** The marginal cost trap in *HWYMYL* is WYSIATI applied to decisions. System 1 evaluates only the immediate cost ("just this once") and doesn't model the cumulative identity damage. Full-cost thinking is a System 2 discipline.

## Key Quotes

> "The most dangerous question a manager can ask is, 'How can we kill this project?' The right question is, 'What assumptions must prove true for this to succeed?'"

> "The reason why it is so difficult for existing firms to capitalise on disruptive innovations is that their processes and their business model that make them good at the existing business actually make them bad at competing for the disruption."

> "100% of the time is easier than 98% of the time."

> "The marginal cost of doing something wrong 'just this once' always seems low."

> "Don't worry about the level of individual prominence you have achieved; worry about the individuals you have helped become better people."
