# Andy Grove

Andrew Stephen Grove (born András István Gróf, 1936–2016) was a Hungarian-American engineer, businessman, and author. He was the third employee and eventual CEO of Intel, guiding it through its transformation from a memory chip company into the world's dominant microprocessor manufacturer. He is widely regarded as one of the greatest managers in Silicon Valley history.

## Background

Grove survived Nazi occupation of Budapest as a child and fled Hungary after the 1956 Soviet invasion, eventually emigrating to the United States. He earned a PhD in chemical engineering from UC Berkeley and joined Robert Noyce and Gordon Moore as a co-founder of Intel in 1968. He became President in 1979, CEO in 1987, and Chairman in 1997.

## Management Philosophy

Grove's management thinking is grounded in systems thinking, operational rigour, and an unflinching focus on output rather than activity. His central insight — that a manager's output equals the output of their team plus the output of adjacent teams they influence — reframes management as a leverage problem, not a presence problem.

Key pillars of his philosophy:

- **[Managerial Leverage](../concepts/managerial-leverage.md):** Identify and spend time on high-leverage activities. Anything that affects the work of many people or that unblocks a key bottleneck is high leverage.
- **[Task-Relevant Maturity](../concepts/task-relevant-maturity.md):** Match management style to the readiness of the employee for a specific task. One style does not fit all.
- **[Output vs Activity](../concepts/output-vs-activity.md):** Managers are measured by results, not effort or hours worked.
- **[One-on-Ones](../concepts/one-on-ones.md):** Grove formalised the one-on-one as a management tool — a regular, structured meeting owned by the subordinate, primarily for information transfer and coaching.
- **Meetings as work:** Grove argued that meetings are not interruptions to work — they *are* the work of a manager.
- **Performance reviews:** The most important management leverage tool. Done well, they are the highest-leverage activity a manager performs.

## OKRs (Objectives and Key Results)

Grove invented the goal-setting system he called **iMBOs** (Intel Management by Objectives). [John Doerr](john-doerr.md) learned the system as an Intel engineer and later introduced it to Google, where it spread across Silicon Valley as [OKRs](../concepts/okrs.md). Grove's version was characterised by aggressive, stretch goals, transparent cascading through the organisation, and a clear separation between objectives (qualitative direction) and key results (quantitative proof).

## Strategic Inflection Points

In *Only the Paranoid Survive* (1996), Grove introduced the concept of the **[Strategic Inflection Point](../concepts/strategic-inflection-points.md)** — a moment when a 10x force changes the competitive dynamics of an industry so fundamentally that the old map no longer applies. Grove's experience navigating Intel's exit from DRAM memory chips into microprocessors was the lived case study.

## Influence

- **John Doerr** learned OKRs from Grove at Intel and spread them globally
- **[Ben Horowitz](ben-horowitz.md)** calls *High Output Management* the best management book ever written for practising CEOs
- Grove's output-first framing rhymes with **Donald Reinertsen's** flow economics and throughput focus
- **[Peter Thiel](peter-thiel.md)'s** paranoia-as-competitive-strategy echoes Grove's "only the paranoid survive" thesis
