# Geoffrey Moore

American author, speaker, and venture partner (born 1946). Managing Director at TCG Advisors; Venture Partner at Wildcat Venture Partners. His 1991 book *Crossing the Chasm* is one of the most widely read strategy texts in Silicon Valley — standard reading at almost every technology company.

Moore took Everett Rogers' *Diffusion of Innovations* (1962) framework and added the insight that changed how the technology industry thinks about go-to-market: the adoption curve is not smooth. There is a **chasm** between early adopters and the mainstream, and it kills most products.

> "The chasm is where high-tech fortunes are made or lost."

## Core Contribution

The central insight: **early market success is not evidence of mainstream readiness**. Visionary early adopters and pragmatist mainstream buyers are fundamentally different — different motivations, different references, different risk tolerance, different definitions of success. A product that wins with visionaries will stall with pragmatists unless the company deliberately crosses the gap.

The prescription: **concentrate, don't diversify**. Cross the chasm by targeting a single, narrow beachhead — dominate it, earn pragmatist references, then expand.

## The Key Works

- **[*Crossing the Chasm*](../concepts/crossing-the-chasm.md)** (1991, revised 1999, 2014) — The core framework: technology adoption curve, the chasm, the beachhead strategy, the Whole Product
- ***Inside the Tornado*** (1995) — Post-chasm dynamics: the Tornado (hypergrowth) and Main Street (maturity)
- ***Dealing with Darwin*** (2005) — How established companies innovate through different growth phases
- ***Zone to Win*** (2015) — Portfolio management for established companies facing disruption

## The Technology Adoption Life Cycle

Moore builds on Rogers' five adopter segments — Innovators, Early Adopters, Early Majority, Late Majority, Laggards — but reveals the **discontinuities** between them. See [Crossing the Chasm](../concepts/crossing-the-chasm.md) for the full model.

The critical discontinuity: **Early Adopters (visionaries) → Early Majority (pragmatists)**. They don't share references. They don't trust each other's endorsements. They want fundamentally different things from a vendor.

## The Beachhead Strategy

To cross the chasm, Moore prescribes a **D-Day military metaphor**: concentrate all force on a single, winnable beachhead segment — not because it's the biggest opportunity, but because:
1. You can dominate it completely
2. Pragmatists in that segment will reference each other
3. Winning it creates a replicable template for adjacent segments

The goal of beachhead marketing: not revenue — **references**. Pragmatists buy from proven vendors. You become proven by winning one segment completely.

## Contrast with Other Thinkers

| Dimension | Moore | [Christensen](./clayton-christensen.md) | [Prahalad](./ck-prahalad.md) | [Bezos](./jeff-bezos.md) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **On entering new markets** | Beachhead: concentrate on one narrow segment | Target non-consumers or low-end; don't attack the core | Compete for future market space; shape the whitespace | Flywheel: start narrow (books), expand via customer trust |
| **On mainstream buyers** | Pragmatists need Whole Product + peer references | They are the incumbents' best customers — the source of disruption blindness | They need to be *created* through industry foresight | Amazon LP: customer obsession, but earn trust by delivery |
| **On product completeness** | Whole Product is mandatory for crossing | RPM alignment required for a new business to work inside a large company | Core competency focus risks leaving the ecosystem incomplete | Working backwards: press release before product — defines the complete promise |
| **On timing** | Cross the chasm before running out of early-market revenue | Disruption happens when incumbents are still profitable | Compete for the future before the future arrives | Day 1 urgency; don't wait until the market forces your hand |

**Key convergence with [Christensen](./clayton-christensen.md):** Christensen's non-consumption targeting is Moore's new-market beachhead from a different angle. Both say: don't attack the incumbent's mainstream customers first. Go where they aren't. The difference: Christensen focuses on *why incumbents can't respond*; Moore focuses on *how the entrant should sequence* its go-to-market.

**Key convergence with [Prahalad](./ck-prahalad.md):** Both observe that early market success can be a trap — it creates the illusion of broader readiness. Prahalad's "industry foresight" is the long-range version of Moore's sequenced expansion: see the full chasm before you're in it.

**Key convergence with [Taleb](./nassim-taleb.md):** The chasm is a [Black Swan](../concepts/black-swan.md) for most startup founders — it's invisible until you're in it, and it doesn't fit the smooth S-curve narrative. The Whole Product requirement mirrors Taleb's critique of naive models that ignore tail dependencies.

**Key convergence with [Reinertsen](./donald-reinertsen.md):** The beachhead strategy is an application of [WIP limits](../concepts/wip-limits.md) to go-to-market: constrain the number of segments you serve simultaneously to maximise flow and build references. Spreading thin is the same error as unlimited WIP.

## Key Quotes

> "The chasm is where high-tech fortunes are made or lost."

> "Pragmatists want to buy from market leaders because market leaders have the most references, the best support, and the lowest adoption risk."

> "The goal of beachhead marketing is not to make money. It is to gain references."

> "In the chasm, the most important word is 'no' — as in, 'No, we will not serve that customer. No, we will not enter that segment. Not yet.'"
