# Paul Graham

> "The most important thing is to be able to think what you want, not to say what you want."

## Who He Is

Paul Graham (b. 1964) is an essayist, programmer, and investor. He co-founded Y Combinator in 2005 with Jessica Livingston and Robert Morris — arguably the most influential startup accelerator in history, which has funded companies including Airbnb, Dropbox, Stripe, and Reddit. Before YC, he founded Viaweb (one of the first web-based applications), which was acquired by Yahoo in 1998. He is also known as a Lisp hacker and author of the books *Hackers & Painters* and *On Lisp*.

His essays at paulgraham.com have been read by tens of millions of people. They span startups, independent thinking, great work, wealth, and how to live well. Graham writes with unusual directness — he says what he actually thinks, which is rare.

## Core Ideas

### On Startups

- **Founder Mode vs Manager Mode:** The conventional wisdom about how to scale companies ("hire good people and give them room") is advice for professional managers, not founders. Founders have different superpowers — the ability to go deep, to do skip-level meetings, to be personally invested — and suppressing these is a mistake.
- **Default Alive or Default Dead:** The key question every startup past early stage must answer: given current burn and growth, do you reach profitability on existing capital? Most founders don't know the answer, which is itself dangerous.
- **Do What Users Want:** The core of all startup advice reduces to this. Founders who talk to users and build what they ask for win. Founders who build what they imagine users want, lose.
- **Be Good:** "Be good" is not just ethical advice — it's strategic. Benevolent startups have better morale, attract better people, get help from others, and have a compass for hard decisions. "Make something people want" is nearly equivalent to "be a charity."
- **Launch Early:** Get something minimal in front of users fast. The information you get from users is more valuable than features you built without them. No startup has ever died from launching too early.

### On Great Work

- **Curiosity as engine:** The recipe for great work starts with finding what you're genuinely curious about — not what seems prestigious, not what the market wants, but what you would think about even if no one paid you.
- **Superlinear Returns:** Performance doesn't scale linearly — it scales exponentially or with threshold effects. Slightly better work can yield disproportionately better outcomes. This means the return on pushing from good to great is enormous.
- **Bus Ticket Theory of Genius:** Genius is not rare talent — it is a disinterested obsession with something that matters. Darwin, Ramanujan, and great hackers all had the intensity of a bus ticket collector. What separated them was that their obsession turned out to be significant.
- **Early Work:** The biggest obstacle to great work is fear of making something lame. But all great projects go through an embarrassing early phase. The ability to push through this phase — to take early work seriously even when it looks rough — is essential.

### On Independent Thinking

- **Moral Fashions:** Every era has taboos — things you can't say without being punished. Most people assume their era has gotten it right. It hasn't. The history of moral progress is a history of heresy becoming orthodoxy. Train yourself to notice the structure of fashionable thinking.
- **Keep Your Identity Small:** The more labels you attach to yourself, the worse you think. When a topic becomes part of your identity, you can no longer reason about it. Politics and religion are destructive to thought not because they're complicated but because people make them part of who they are.
- **Four Quadrants of Conformism:** People differ in both their independence of thought (conventional vs. independent) and the aggressiveness with which they enforce or resist conformity. The aggressively independent-minded are most useful to society; the aggressively conventional-minded are most dangerous.

### On Wealth

- **How People Get Rich Now:** In 1982, wealth came mostly from inheritance, oil, and real estate. By 2020, it comes mostly from starting companies. Technology has made it possible for individuals to create enormous value with small teams.
- **Inequality and Risk:** Economic inequality is often the footprint of risk-taking. The way to reduce the worst outcomes of inequality is not to suppress risk-taking but to ensure more people can participate in it.

## Relation to Other Thinkers

- **[Peter Thiel](peter-thiel.md):** Both are contrarian thinkers on startups. Thiel focuses on monopoly and secrets; Graham focuses on users and iteration. Thiel is more macro/strategic; Graham is more micro/tactical. Graham's "do what users want" vs. Thiel's "find a secret" are complementary entry points to startup thinking.
- **[Naval Ravikant](naval-ravikant.md):** Both think deeply about wealth and independent thinking. Naval is more aphoristic; Graham is more analytical. Both emphasize specific knowledge (Naval) / curiosity-driven work (Graham) over credentials. Both see the internet as an era-defining leverage multiplier.
- **[Ben Horowitz](ben-horowitz.md):** Horowitz is the other great essayist of startup leadership. Graham writes more from the founder's early experience; Horowitz from the CEO's scaling experience. Graham's "Founder Mode" and Horowitz's *The Hard Thing About Hard Things* are natural companions.
