# Mental Models for Decision-Making

A navigational map of the decision-making and psychology content in this wiki. These frameworks answer: *why do smart people make bad decisions, and how do you make better ones?*

---

## The Core Problem: Your Brain Misleads You

The starting point:

> **System 1 is fast, automatic, and wrong in predictable ways. System 2 is slow, deliberate, and usually not engaged.**

[Daniel Kahneman's](../people/daniel-kahneman.md) decades of research showed that human judgment is systematically biased — not randomly, but in patterned, exploitable ways. [Robert Cialdini](../people/robert-cialdini.md) showed exactly how those patterns are exploited. [Nassim Taleb](../people/nassim-taleb.md) showed what happens when institutions built on biased human judgment encounter rare, extreme events.

The good news: the biases are *predictable*. You can build systems, habits, and practices that partially override them.

## The Kahneman → Cialdini Bridge

Every one of Cialdini's 7 principles is an exploitation of a Kahneman System 1 mechanism:

| Cialdini Principle | Kahneman Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Social Proof | WYSIATI + availability heuristic |
| Authority | Halo effect |
| Scarcity | Loss aversion |
| Commitment | Anchoring + consistency bias |
| Liking | Affect heuristic |
| Reciprocity | Fairness norm |
| Unity | In-group bias |

Kahneman explains *why* the architecture exists (evolutionary). Cialdini maps *how* it's triggered. Together they give you both diagnosis and defence.

## Practical Decision-Making Sequence

**Before a major decision:**
1. Name the cognitive biases most likely to be active (availability? anchoring? commitment?)
2. Take the [outside view](../concepts/planning-fallacy.md) — what do reference class outcomes look like?
3. Pre-mortem: assume the decision failed; what went wrong?
4. Who has [skin in the game](../concepts/skin-in-the-game.md) here? Weight advice accordingly.

**In a negotiation or influence situation:**
1. Identify which Cialdini principles are being activated (reciprocity? scarcity? authority?)
2. Separate the offer from the rapport — would you accept this from someone you don't like?
3. Resist artificial time pressure ([scarcity](../concepts/scarcity.md)) by insisting on time to think.

**In an organisation:**
1. Is information flowing freely? ([Westrum](../concepts/westrum-cultural-typologies.md) — what culture type are you in?)
2. Are decisions being made by those with the best information, or the most seniority? ([Idea Meritocracy](../concepts/idea-meritocracy.md))
3. What mental model is the team using? Is it explicit and testable? ([Learning Organisation](../concepts/learning-organisation.md))

## Key Insight: Structural vs. Cognitive Fixes

Most decision-making advice focuses on *thinking harder* — be more rational, watch for biases, slow down. Kahneman himself is sceptical that individual cognitive effort reliably overcomes System 1.

The more robust fixes are **structural**:
- Dalio's radical transparency changes the *information environment* rather than trying to change individual cognition
- Taleb's skin in the game changes *incentives* rather than asking for better judgment
- Westrum's generative culture changes *what information surfaces* rather than hoping individuals will speak up voluntarily
- OKRs change *what gets measured* rather than hoping people will track the right things intuitively

The theme: change the system, not just the person. This is Meadows' [Leverage Points](../concepts/leverage-points.md) insight applied to decision-making.

## Paul Graham on Thinking Freely

Graham's contribution to decision-making is not about cognitive debiasing — it's about epistemic courage: the willingness to notice when the crowd is wrong and to think thoughts that are socially costly to hold. These essays address a failure mode Kahneman doesn't: not *irrational* thinking but *conformist* thinking.

| Essay | Core insight |
|---|---|
| [What You Can't Say](../sources/pg-say.md) | Every era has moral fashions — unsayable beliefs that may be true. The skill is learning to notice them, and to think them privately even when you can't say them publicly |
| [The Four Quadrants of Conformism](../sources/pg-conformism.md) | People vary in how conventional vs. independent their thinking is, and how aggressively they enforce it. The aggressively conventional are the most dangerous; the aggressively independent are the most valuable in uncertain domains |
| [Heresy](../sources/pg-heresy.md) | The mechanism of suppression: heresy treats empirical incorrectness as moral wickedness, collapsing the distinction between "is X true?" and "is the person who says X good?" |
| [Keep Your Identity Small](../sources/pg-identity.md) | Identity is a decision-making failure mode: the more a topic becomes part of who you are, the worse your reasoning about it. The fix is to keep your identity as label-free as possible |
| [How to Think for Yourself](../sources/pg-think.md) | Why independent-mindedness is a prerequisite for ambitious work; how to cultivate it; the habits that distinguish independent from conventional thinkers |

### Where Graham fits alongside Kahneman

Kahneman addresses *automatic* failures of thinking (heuristics, biases, System 1 overreach). Graham addresses *social* failures of thinking (conformism, identity, moral fashion, heresy). Both matter:

- You can be fully unbiased by Kahneman's standard and still think mostly what your tribe thinks
- Graham's independent-minded person still needs Kahneman's toolkit for individual judgment
- The combination: think free *and* think clearly
