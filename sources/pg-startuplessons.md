# "The Hardest Lessons for Startups to Learn" — Paul Graham (2006)

## Bibliographic Info

- **Author:** Paul Graham
- **Published:** April 2006
- **Origin:** Talk at Startup School 2006; expanded to essay

## Central Argument

Certain startup lessons keep having to be repeated because they are **counterintuitive** — they contradict what people learn in school, at big companies, and from conventional business wisdom. The lessons that have to be repeated the most are the counterintuitive ones. Absorbing them early can be the difference between life and death for a startup.

## Key Ideas

1. **Release Early.** Get a minimal version in front of users fast, then iterate based on their feedback. Bugs are forgivable; a minimal version 1 is fine. No startup has ever died from releasing too early; many have died from being too slow. You don't know your users until you have them.
2. **Do What Users Want.** This seems obvious but is surprisingly rare. Most founders build what they imagine users want. Real success comes from talking to users, watching what they do, and building what they actually need. "Doing what users want" is an algorithm that scales across all decisions.
3. **Spend Little.** Startups that are cheap to run don't need to raise money desperately and don't have to compromise to get it. Cheapness is a superpower: it buys you time to figure out the right thing to build. "The less you spend, the more options you have."
4. **Focus.** With a small team and hundreds of things to do, focus is critical. Founders who spread their energy across many things fail. Pick the most important thing and work on it exclusively until it's done or definitively isn't working.
5. **Don't Raise Too Much.** More money means more burn, more employees, more coordination overhead, more pressure to scale a product that isn't ready. Many startups raised money and killed themselves with it. The goal is to find product-market fit, not to grow a big organization.
6. **Build a Real Business.** The best startups are driven by something more than money. Founders who genuinely want to help users build something lasting. Mercenary founders take the first exit.
7. **Persistence Matters More Than You Think.** Most startups that succeed went through long periods when it looked like they would fail. The companies that survive are the ones that didn't give up during those periods. Not giving up is itself a strategy.

## Notable Quotes

> "I've seen a lot of startups die because they were too slow to release stuff, and none because they were too quick."

> "The way to succeed in a startup is not to be an expert on startups, but to be an expert on your users and the problem you're solving for them."

> "The less you spend, the more options you have. The less you spend, the easier it is to get profitable, which gives you more options still."

> "Persistence is the most important quality in a startup, and a lot of what determines it is morale."
