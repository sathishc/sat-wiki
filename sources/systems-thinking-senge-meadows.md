## What Is Systems Thinking?

Systems thinking is a way of understanding reality that emphasises **relationships, feedback, and emergence** over linear cause-and-effect. Rather than asking "what caused this?" it asks "what feedback structures are producing this behaviour?"

The core insight: **most problems that resist solution are not caused by isolated events or bad actors — they are produced by the structure of the system itself.** Changing the actors without changing the structure produces the same behaviour.

## Donella Meadows — Thinking in Systems

### Core Components of a System

Every system has three components:

1. **Stocks** — the elements you can see, feel, count, or measure at a given moment (water in a bathtub, money in a bank account, trust in a relationship, population)
2. **Flows** — the rates that change stocks over time (inflows and outflows: births/deaths, income/spending, building trust/eroding trust)
3. **Feedback loops** — the mechanism by which a change in a stock affects the flows that change that stock

**The key insight:** You cannot understand system behaviour by examining stocks alone. The behaviour emerges from the feedback structure — the loops that connect stocks and flows.

### Feedback Loops

**Reinforcing (positive) feedback loops:** A change in a stock amplifies further change in the same direction. Also called "virtuous cycles" or "vicious cycles" depending on direction.
- Bank interest: money → interest → more money
- Population growth: people → births → more people
- Network effects: users → value → more users
- Amazon flywheel: customer experience → traffic → sellers → selection → lower costs → lower prices → more customer experience

These loops produce **exponential growth or collapse**. They don't self-correct.

**Balancing (negative) feedback loops:** A change in a stock triggers a response that opposes that change. These loops seek a goal or equilibrium.
- Thermostat: temperature below target → furnace on → temperature rises → furnace off
- Inventory management: inventory too low → order more → inventory rises
- Price mechanism: price rises → demand falls → price falls

These loops produce **stability and goal-seeking behaviour**.

**The key insight:** Most complex system behaviour comes from the interplay of multiple reinforcing and balancing loops operating simultaneously at different time scales.

### Delays

Delays in feedback loops are a primary source of system dysfunction. When the feedback signal is delayed relative to the action:
- Decision-makers overshoot (keep taking an action after it has already produced the desired effect, because the effect hasn't shown up yet)
- Oscillation occurs (boom-bust cycles, overshoot-and-collapse)
- The system appears unstable even though its structure is deterministic

Examples:
- Policy implementation: government intervenes in the economy, but effects are visible 12–18 months later → overshoot
- Construction lag: demand for new buildings rises → construction starts → market responds with oversupply 3 years later
- Species population: predator eats prey → prey population falls → predator population collapses (delayed feedback)

### Leverage Points

Meadows' most influential contribution: a hierarchy of **places to intervene in a system**, ordered from least to most effective:

| Level | Intervention | Effectiveness |
|---|---|---|
| 12 | Numbers (constants, parameters) | Least effective — tweaking settings rarely changes behaviour |
| 11 | Sizes of buffers/stocks | Often physically impossible to change quickly |
| 10 | Structure of material flows | Sometimes can change; hard to restructure |
| 9 | Lengths of delays | High leverage if you can change them |
| 8 | Strength of negative feedback loops | Improves goal-seeking |
| 7 | Gain of driving positive feedback | High leverage; slowing a reinforcing loop |
| 6 | Structure of information flows | Very high; who gets what information |
| 5 | Rules of the system | Laws, incentives, constraints |
| 4 | Power to change the rules | Who sets the rules |
| 3 | Goals of the system | What the system is trying to achieve |
| 2 | Mindset / paradigm | The shared assumptions behind the system |
| 1 | Power to change paradigms | Highest leverage of all |

Most policy interventions target the bottom of this list (tweaking numbers). The highest leverage comes from changing information flows, rules, goals, and paradigms — which are also the most resisted changes.

### Archetypes

Meadows (and Senge) identified recurring system structures that produce recognisable behaviour patterns:

**Limits to Growth:** A reinforcing loop drives growth until it runs into a balancing constraint. If the constraint is ignored or pushed against (rather than eased), collapse follows faster.

**Shifting the Burden:** A symptom is relieved by a symptomatic solution (quick fix), which reduces pressure to address the fundamental solution. The quick fix strengthens; the fundamental problem atrophies.

**Tragedy of the Commons:** Multiple actors draw from a shared resource. Each individual's rational action degrades the resource for all. No single actor has incentive to restrain.

**Escalation:** Two balancing loops competing: one actor's increase triggers the other's increase, which triggers the first's further increase. Arms races, price wars, advertising wars.

**Success to the Successful:** Two activities compete for limited resources. The one that is initially more successful receives more resources, which makes it more successful, which further skews resource allocation.

## Peter Senge — The Fifth Discipline

Senge applied systems thinking specifically to organisations and learning. His core claim: the primary failure of organisations is an inability to learn — and systems thinking is the meta-discipline that enables learning.

### The Five Disciplines

1. **Personal Mastery** — continuous individual development; knowing what you truly want (vision) and seeing current reality clearly
2. **Mental Models** — the assumptions and generalisations that shape how we understand the world; making them explicit and testable
3. **Shared Vision** — a genuine, shared picture of the future that creates motivation and alignment (not a vision statement imposed from the top)
4. **Team Learning** — the capacity for a team to think together; dialogue vs. discussion; suspending assumptions
5. **Systems Thinking** — the Fifth Discipline; the integrating framework that makes the other four cohere

The name "fifth discipline" signals primacy: systems thinking is the one that enables the others to work together. Without it, the other disciplines are isolated improvements; with it, they become mutually reinforcing.

### Learning Disabilities in Organisations

Senge identifies seven organisational learning disabilities — structural problems that prevent learning:

1. **"I am my position"** — people identify with their job, not the system they're part of; can't see broader consequences of actions
2. **"The enemy is out there"** — problems are attributed to external actors; the system's own contribution is invisible
3. **The illusion of taking charge** — proactiveness is actually reactiveness in disguise; attacking symptoms not causes
4. **The fixation on events** — attention is on discrete events, not the gradual processes beneath them
5. **The parable of the boiled frog** — slow, gradual threats are not perceived until it is too late
6. **The delusion of learning from experience** — we learn from direct feedback, but most consequences of major decisions are delayed beyond our learning horizon
7. **The myth of the management team** — the management team projects unity while spending its collective energy managing the appearance of having it together

### Dialogue vs. Discussion

Senge draws a sharp distinction (following Bohm):
- **Discussion:** Advocacy; people push their views; the goal is to win
- **Dialogue:** Collective inquiry; people suspend their assumptions; the goal is to understand together

Learning organisations require dialogue. Most organisations practice only discussion — which reinforces existing mental models rather than updating them.

### Archetypes in Organisations

**Shifting the Burden** (most common): An operational problem produces a symptom. A quick fix (management intervention, cost-cutting, a new hire) relieves the symptom. The underlying problem is never addressed. Reliance on quick fixes grows. Capacity to address the fundamental problem erodes.

**Limits to Growth in sales**: A product grows through a reinforcing loop (word of mouth, network effects). Growth encounters a constraint (service quality, delivery capacity, cultural fit). Management pushes the reinforcing loop (more salespeople, more marketing) instead of addressing the constraint. Growth slows; the hard push produces diminishing returns.

## System Dynamics vs. Soft Systems

**System Dynamics (Forrester/Meadows):** Quantitative modelling of stocks, flows, and feedback loops. Can build simulation models. Best for well-defined systems with measurable flows (supply chains, population dynamics, epidemiology).

**Soft Systems Methodology (Checkland):** For "messy" human and organisational systems where the problem itself is unclear. Focuses on understanding different worldviews (Weltanschauungen) rather than modelling physical flows. Less about equations; more about surfacing assumptions.

## Key Quotes

**Meadows:**
> "Systems thinking is not about thinking more about the future. It is about being less surprised by it."
> "You think that because you understand 'one' that you must therefore understand 'two' because one and one make two. But you forget that you must also understand 'and'."
> "The most important systems wisdom I know is this: before you disturb a system, understand it."

**Senge:**
> "The learning organisation is an organisation that is continually expanding its capacity to create its future."
> "Today's problems come from yesterday's solutions."
> "We learn best from experience, but we never directly experience the consequences of many of our most important decisions."
> "Structure influences behaviour. Different people in the same structure tend to produce qualitatively similar results."
