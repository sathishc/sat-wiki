# Think Again

**Author:** Adam Grant  
**Published:** 2021  
**Publisher:** Viking / Penguin  
**Type:** Non-fiction, organisational psychology / epistemics

---

## Summary

*Think Again* makes the case that **rethinking is an undervalued skill** — the ability to recognise when your beliefs are wrong, update on new evidence, and hold your views loosely without losing confidence. In a fast-changing world, the ability to re-examine what you think you know is more valuable than the knowledge itself.

Grant's central argument: most people default to defending, attacking, or performing when their beliefs are challenged. The best thinkers — in science, business, and life — default to the scientist mode instead.

---

## Key Concepts

### The Four Mindsets

| Mindset | Mode | Behaviour |
|---------|------|-----------|
| **Preacher** | Defending | Protect your beliefs; treat challenges as threats to your identity |
| **Prosecutor** | Attacking | Win arguments by dismantling the other side; focused on being right |
| **Politician** | Performing | Seek approval; adjust positions to match the audience |
| **Scientist** | Learning | Form hypotheses; test them; update on evidence; curiosity over conviction |

The Preacher, Prosecutor, and Politician are not bad people — these are **modes** we all slip into, often without noticing. The Scientist mode is a deliberate practice.

### Intellectual Humility
Knowing what you don't know, and being genuinely willing to change. This is distinct from low confidence: you can hold strong views and be highly capable while still being humble about the limits of your knowledge and the possibility of being wrong.

Grant introduces the concept of **confident humility**: strong skills and clear positions, combined with genuine openness to updating them. "I have conviction in my approach *and* I know I might be wrong."

### The Rethinking Cycle
Intellectual humility → curiosity → discovery → rethinking. The cycle is self-reinforcing: the more you practise updating, the less threatening it feels to be wrong, and the easier it becomes to notice when you are.

### Challenge Networks
A **challenge network** is a set of people who care about you *and* who will challenge your thinking — not to undermine you, but to stress-test your ideas. Distinct from a support network (which validates) and an adversarial critic (who wants you to fail). Challenge network members are productive dissenters: they point out the flaws others won't mention.

Grant's own research: the most impactful feedback on his writing came from people who liked him *and* pushed back hard on weak arguments.

### Motivational Interviewing as Rethinking Tool
Rather than directly confronting someone's incorrect belief (which triggers defensiveness), Grant draws on the clinical technique of motivational interviewing: ask questions that help the person discover their own reasons to change. "What would convince you that you might be wrong?" People who discover their own doubts are far more likely to update than those who are told they're wrong.

### Complexity of Issues
One of Grant's findings: when you acknowledge the complexity and downsides of your own position, people find you more credible — not less. Confident humility in arguments is more persuasive than one-sided advocacy.
