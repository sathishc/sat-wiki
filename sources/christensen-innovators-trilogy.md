## The Innovator's Dilemma (1997)

Core thesis: Good management — doing everything "right" — can still destroy a company.

Successful companies fail not because they're poorly managed, but because they're *well* managed according to conventional logic. They listen to their best customers, invest in sustaining innovations, and ignore low-end or fringe markets that don't look profitable.

Meanwhile, disruptive innovators enter from below — offering cheaper, simpler, "good enough" products to ignored or non-consuming customers. Incumbents ignore them (rational, given financials). Then disruptors improve rapidly and eat the market from underneath.

### Sustaining vs. Disruptive Innovation

- **Sustaining innovation:** Better products for existing customers. Incumbents win.
- **Disruptive innovation:** Simpler/cheaper for new or low-end customers. New entrants win.

### Key Mechanisms

- **Value networks:** Companies are embedded in networks (customers, investors, cost structures) that punish deviation. The network's logic makes ignoring disruption rational at every step.
- **Resource allocation:** Middle managers kill disruptive projects — they don't fit existing metrics, margins, or customer demands.
- **Overshooting:** Incumbents keep improving beyond what most customers need, creating room for "good enough" alternatives.

### The Dilemma

Managers know the principles of good management. Following them is precisely what causes failure. The dilemma: the rational decision at each step leads to an irrational outcome overall.

### Classic Examples

- **Disk drives:** 14" → 8" → 5.25" → 3.5" — each transition led by a new entrant
- **Steel minimills** vs. integrated steel mills (entered at low-margin rebar, worked up)
- **Honda** entering US motorcycle market via small bikes (Supercub) while incumbents focused on heavyweights
- Digital photography vs. film; Netflix vs. Blockbuster; PCs vs. mainframes

## The Innovator's Solution (2003)

Co-authored with Michael Raynor.

Core thesis: A prescription, not just a diagnosis. How do you become the disruptor rather than the disrupted?

### Jobs to Be Done (JTBD)

Most influential concept. Customers don't buy products — they *hire* them to do a job. Understanding the job (functional, emotional, social dimensions) is more predictive than demographics.

McDonald's milkshakes example: morning commuters hired milkshakes for a different job (long commute, one-handed, slowly satisfying) than afternoon families (treat for kids). Same product, entirely different jobs. Product design should follow the job.

### How to Pick Disruptive Battles

- Target **non-consumption** (people not using existing solutions because too expensive/complicated)
- Or target **low-end footholds** (customers overserved by incumbents)
- Don't aim at the incumbent's best customers first — let performance improvements pull you up-market over time

### The RPM Framework

- **Resources** — what a company has (capital, people, technology)
- **Processes** — how it does things (routines, workflows, culture)
- **Values** — what it prioritises (margins, customers, metrics)

Disruption often fails inside incumbents because processes and values are incompatible with the new business model, even when resources are abundant.

### Integration vs. Modularisation

- **Integrate** (do more in-house) when performance isn't yet good enough for the market
- **Modularise/outsource** when performance exceeds what customers need
- Determines where profit pools migrate in a value chain

### Avoiding Commoditisation

Profit migrates to the "not good enough" layer. Companies must identify which layer in the value chain is underperforming customer needs and control it.

### Practical Guidance

- Create separate organisational units for disruptive businesses — don't embed them in the parent
- Hire managers whose *experiences* (not just skills) match the challenges ahead
- Think carefully: build vs. buy vs. borrow

## How Will You Measure Your Life? (2012)

Co-authored with James Allworth and Karen Dillon. Adapted from a Harvard Business School commencement speech.

Core thesis: The same rigorous frameworks used to analyse businesses can help you build a meaningful life.

Christensen watched brilliant HBS classmates become professionally successful and personally wrecked — divorced, estranged from kids, a few in prison. Jeffrey Skilling of Enron was a classmate. This book is his attempt to explain the mechanism.

### Three Questions

1. How can I be sure I'll be happy in my career?
2. How can I be sure my relationships with family and close friends become an enduring source of happiness?
3. How can I stay out of jail? (About integrity and the marginal cost trap)

### Career & Motivation — Herzberg's Two-Factor Theory

- **Hygiene factors** (salary, status, security): absence causes dissatisfaction; presence does NOT create motivation
- **Motivators** (challenge, growth, meaning, autonomy): these actually drive engagement

The trap: people optimise for hygiene factors (chasing salary, prestige) and wonder why they feel empty.

**Emergent vs. deliberate strategy:** Be open to unexpected opportunities early in career. Have hypotheses, test them. Career strategy should be *discovered*, not just planned.

### Relationships & Family

**The marginal cost fallacy in relationships:**
- The marginal cost of "just this once" (skipping dinner, missing a recital) seems low but compounds devastatingly
- Same as a company's "just this once" exceptions that become strategic drift

**Jobs to Be Done for relationships:**
Understand what job your spouse/children are actually hiring *you* to do. It may not be what you think.

**The School of Experience:**
Children need challenges and the chance to fail — overprotection robs them of the experiences that build capability. Hire/develop for the right experiences, not just credentials.

### The Marginal Cost Trap & Integrity

One of the book's most powerful passages:

> "The marginal cost of doing something wrong 'just this once' always seems low. It succinctly explains how good people with strong values can slip into bad behaviour and ruin their lives and careers."

Integrity is not a policy you implement each time. It's a commitment you make *once* — that you will never compromise it. **100% commitment is easier than 98% commitment.** The 2% exception opens the door.

Full cost vs. marginal cost: Always evaluate the *real* cost of decisions, including identity and reputational cost, not just the incremental cost.

### The Metric

On your deathbed, you won't measure your life in revenue or accolades. The metric will be the *individual lives you've touched* — the depth of your relationships, the people you helped become better.

> "The most powerful statement of love is helping another person develop their own potential."

## The Throughline Across All Three

Good intentions + wrong theory = bad outcomes.

Whether it's a company listening too hard to its best customers, or a parent too busy to attend their kid's events — the mechanism is the same. Incentives, marginal cost thinking, and misaligned metrics lead smart, well-meaning people astray. Christensen's gift was making that legible — and offering a way out.
