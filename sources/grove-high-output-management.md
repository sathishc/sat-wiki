# High Output Management

**Author:** Andy Grove  
**First published:** 1983  
**Updated edition:** 1995  
**Publisher:** Random House

## Overview

*High Output Management* is Andy Grove's systematic treatment of what a manager's job actually is. Written during his tenure running Intel, it is unusual among management books in being both deeply practical and grounded in an engineering mindset — treating management as a process with measurable outputs, bottlenecks, and leverage points.

Ben Horowitz has called it the best management book he has ever read. Its influence runs through the OKR movement, the one-on-one as a standard management practice, and the broader shift toward output-based rather than activity-based performance thinking in Silicon Valley.

## Core Thesis

A manager's output is not their personal work product. It is the **output of their team** plus the **output of adjacent teams they influence**. This means that management is fundamentally about leverage — identifying activities that multiply the work of others — rather than personal productivity.

## Key Concepts

### Managerial Output and Leverage

The central framework: managerial output = team output + influenced output. Since a manager's time is fixed, the job is to allocate it to the **highest-leverage activities**: actions that affect many people, unblock critical paths, or create lasting improvements to how the team works.

→ See: [Managerial Leverage](../concepts/managerial-leverage.md)

### Task-Relevant Maturity

Management style should not be fixed. It should vary based on the employee's **task-relevant maturity** (TRM) — their experience and readiness for *this specific task*:

- Low TRM → directive (tell them what to do)
- Medium TRM → coaching (explain the why, invite input)
- High TRM → delegating (set the objective, get out of the way)

→ See: [Task-Relevant Maturity](../concepts/task-relevant-maturity.md)

### Output vs Activity

The manager is judged by output, not effort. "Being busy" is not a defence. This reframes the manager's entire self-assessment — the question is never "did I work hard?" but "did my team produce more?"

→ See: [Output vs Activity](../concepts/output-vs-activity.md)

### One-on-Ones

Grove formalised the one-on-one meeting as a core management tool. The meeting is *owned by the subordinate*, primarily serves information transfer upward, and is a primary vehicle for coaching and problem-solving. Frequency matters: weekly for most direct reports.

→ See: [One-on-Ones](../concepts/one-on-ones.md)

### Meetings as Work

Grove pushes back against the idea that meetings are unproductive. For a manager, meetings *are* the primary production mechanism — the means by which decisions are made, information flows, and work is coordinated. The distinction is between **process-oriented meetings** (recurring, operational) and **mission-oriented meetings** (ad hoc, decision-focused).

### Performance Reviews

Grove treats the performance review as the single highest-leverage activity a manager performs. A well-done review shapes an employee's understanding of their work, their trajectory, and what excellence looks like. It should be candid, specific, and frequent enough to be actionable.

### Motivation and the Two-Variable Model

Grove draws on Maslow and Herzberg to argue that employee performance is a product of both **motivation** and **capability**. A fully capable but unmotivated employee performs poorly; a highly motivated but undertrained one does too. The manager's job is to raise both.

### OKRs (iMBOs)

Grove's internal system — Intel Management by Objectives — is the direct ancestor of OKRs. The key features: aggressive stretch goals, transparent cascading, and a hard distinction between objectives (direction) and key results (measurable outcomes).

→ See: [OKRs](../concepts/okrs.md)

## Influence and Legacy

- **John Doerr** absorbed Grove's iMBO system at Intel and re-introduced it to Google as OKRs
- **Ben Horowitz** cites this book as the foundational management text for founders
- The book's output-first framing directly influenced how performance management is approached in high-growth tech companies
