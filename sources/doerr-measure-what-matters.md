## Measure What Matters (2018)

John Doerr's book is part history, part manual. It traces how OKRs (Objectives and Key Results) were invented by Andy Grove at Intel, carried by Doerr to Google in 1999, and subsequently spread across Silicon Valley and beyond. The book is structured around case studies — Google, Intel, Bono's ONE campaign, the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation, Adobe — with Doerr explaining the OKR mechanics through each.

The core claim: OKRs are a management system that can transform how organisations set and achieve goals — providing focus, alignment, accountability, and a culture that distinguishes ambition from execution.

---

## The Origin: Andy Grove at Intel

OKRs were developed by Andy Grove (author of *High Output Management*) at Intel in the 1970s as a response to a specific problem: Intel was pivoting from memory chips to microprocessors, required extreme execution precision, and needed a way to ensure the entire organisation was moving in the same direction simultaneously.

Grove's insight: **what gets measured gets managed**. And more specifically: separate the *objective* (what you want to achieve) from the *key results* (how you will measure whether you achieved it).

Doerr was a young engineer at Intel when Grove was running OKRs, learned the system there, and later introduced it to Larry Page and Sergey Brin at Google in a 90-minute presentation using a single PowerPoint deck. They adopted it; Google has used OKRs ever since.

## The Core Framework

### Objectives

An Objective is:
- **Qualitative** — inspirational, directional; describes *what* you want to achieve
- **Significant** — should matter; not business-as-usual
- **Action-oriented** — uses strong verbs; creates momentum
- **Time-bound** — typically quarterly, sometimes annual
- **Aspirational** — should create a little discomfort; stretch without being absurd

Examples:
- "Build a product users love" (not: "Improve user satisfaction by 10%")
- "Become the undisputed leader in machine learning"
- "Create a recruiting process that attracts world-class talent"

The objective answers: *Where do I want to go?*

### Key Results

Key Results are:
- **Quantitative** — specific, measurable, verifiable; binary (done/not done) or numeric
- **Aggressive but realistic** — not trivially achievable; not impossible
- **Time-bound** — same period as the objective
- **Outcomes, not activities** — "launch X feature" is an activity; "30% of users engage with X feature" is a key result
- Typically 3–5 per objective

Examples (for "Build a product users love"):
- "Achieve NPS of 50+"
- "Reduce 30-day churn to below 8%"
- "Reach 1M daily active users"

The key results answer: *How will I know I've arrived?*

### The Pairing Discipline

The discipline of writing OKRs is in holding both in tension:
- The Objective provides inspiration and direction
- The Key Results provide rigour and accountability
- Together they prevent both vague ambition (objective without measurement) and metric-gaming (measurement without meaningful direction)

## The Four OKR Superpowers

Doerr identifies four things OKRs do that generic goal-setting does not:

### 1. Focus

OKRs force prioritisation. You can only have a few OKRs (3–5 objectives per cycle, 3–5 key results each). Choosing what to put in forces choosing what to leave out. The act of committing to a small number of objectives is itself a strategic decision.

Grove's maxim: "If we tried to focus on everything, we focus on nothing."

### 2. Alignment

OKRs are typically made public across the organisation. Everyone can see what everyone else is working toward — including leaders. This creates:
- **Vertical alignment** — individuals can align their OKRs to their team's OKRs, which align to company OKRs
- **Horizontal alignment** — teams working on related goals can find each other and coordinate

Doerr recommends roughly 60% bottom-up OKRs (set by individuals/teams) and 40% top-down. Pure top-down cascades destroy ownership; pure bottom-up loses coherence.

### 3. Tracking and Accountability

OKRs are regularly reviewed — weekly check-ins, mid-cycle reviews, end-of-cycle scoring. This creates:
- **Lead measure visibility** — you can see whether you're on track before the deadline
- **Course correction** — if a key result is off track, you know early enough to act
- **Accountability without blame** — the system surfaces reality; the conversation is about what to do next

Key Results are scored 0.0–1.0 at cycle end:
- 0.7–1.0: delivered
- 0.4–0.6: progress but incomplete
- 0.0–0.3: failed to make progress

Doerr (following Grove) considers a consistent score of 1.0 on all OKRs a signal that the objectives weren't ambitious enough.

### 4. Stretching (Moonshots)

OKRs separate **committed OKRs** (must be achieved; operational; failure is not okay) from **aspirational/stretch OKRs** (moonshots; expected score ~0.7; failure to reach 1.0 is fine if progress is real).

Google calls these "Moonshots":
- A moonshot OKR aims for 10x improvement, not 10%
- The scoring expectation is ~0.7 (if you're regularly hitting 1.0, you weren't aiming high enough)
- Teams are not penalised for ambitious OKRs that score 0.6

This is critical: if all OKRs are evaluated as pass/fail, people will game them — set easy targets to ensure 1.0s. The moonshot culture separates ambition from operational reliability.

## CFRs: The Companion System

OKRs work best alongside a parallel practice Doerr calls **CFRs** — Conversations, Feedback, Recognition:

- **Conversations:** Regular 1:1s between manager and report focused on goal progress, blockers, development
- **Feedback:** Continuous, specific feedback — not annual reviews
- **Recognition:** Peer-to-peer and manager recognition of OKR contributions

CFRs are the "how" layer — OKRs set direction; CFRs drive the culture and human connection that make execution possible. Doerr argues that organisations that implement OKRs without CFRs often see the system become mechanical and detached.

## Common OKR Failures

Doerr identifies the most common failure modes:

1. **Too many OKRs** — losing focus; trying to track everything
2. **Treating OKRs as a to-do list** — key results as activities (tasks), not outcomes
3. **Set-and-forget** — writing OKRs at the start of the quarter and not reviewing them
4. **Gaming the key results** — setting targets you know you'll hit to protect your score
5. **Disconnected OKRs** — individual OKRs that don't connect to team or company objectives (alignment failure)
6. **Using OKRs for performance reviews** — this causes people to sandbar (set conservative OKRs to protect their rating); OKRs and performance reviews should be separate conversations
7. **Top-down-only cascades** — kills ownership; teams need to own their own OKRs

## OKRs vs. KPIs

| | OKRs | KPIs |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Direction + stretch | Health monitoring |
| Timeframe | Quarterly (mostly) | Ongoing |
| Scope | Change / progress | Steady state |
| Ambition | Aspirational | Operational |
| Failure | Acceptable (moonshots) | Not acceptable |

KPIs are dials on the dashboard — they tell you if the engine is healthy. OKRs are the destination on the GPS — they tell you where you're going. Both are necessary; they answer different questions.
