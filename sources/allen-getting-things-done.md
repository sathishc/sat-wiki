# Getting Things Done — David Allen

**Author:** David Allen
**Published:** 2001 (revised 2015)
**Genre:** Productivity / Personal organisation

## Summary

*Getting Things Done* (GTD) argues that the human brain is for having ideas, not storing them. The core premise: stress and anxiety about open loops — unfinished commitments rattling in your head — can be eliminated by externalising everything into a **trusted system** outside your mind. Once you trust the system, you achieve "mind like water": a calm, responsive state where you can fully engage with whatever is in front of you.

GTD is a complete workflow methodology, not a time-management hack. It works at the level of *capturing* and *clarifying* work, not just scheduling it.

---

## The Five-Step Process (Core Workflow)

GTD's five steps apply to everything that has your attention:

1. **Capture** — Collect every open loop, idea, task, commitment into inboxes (physical trays, notebooks, digital apps). Get it all out of your head. You need as few inboxes as possible, but as many as necessary.

2. **Clarify** — Process what each item *is* and what (if anything) you need to do about it. The key clarifying questions:
   - Is it actionable?
     - **No** → trash it, incubate it (Someday/Maybe list), or file it as reference
     - **Yes** → What is the very next physical action?
       - If it takes <2 minutes: do it now (**Two-Minute Rule**)
       - If someone else should do it: delegate it (Waiting For list)
       - If it's a multi-step outcome: it's a **Project** — capture the desired outcome on the Projects list

3. **Organise** — Put everything in the right place: Next Actions lists (by context), Projects list, Waiting For list, Calendar, Someday/Maybe list, Reference files.

4. **Reflect** — Review your system regularly. The **Weekly Review** is the cornerstone: clear inboxes, review all lists, update Projects, ensure everything has a next action. Without reflection the system degrades.

5. **Engage** — Choose what to do based on context, time available, energy, and priorities. The system provides the trusted inventory; you choose.

## Mind Like Water

A metaphor from martial arts. A still pond responds to a thrown stone with exactly the right force — no more, no less — then returns to stillness. GTD aims to create the same mental state: full responsiveness without over- or under-reaction. You get there when you fully trust your external system.

The opposite of mind like water is the mental chatter of uncaptured open loops ("I must remember to call John…"). Each uncaptured commitment consumes cognitive bandwidth even when you're not working on it.

---

## The Trusted System

The external system where everything lives. Key properties:
- **Complete** — everything is in it; nothing hidden in memory
- **Current** — regularly reviewed and updated
- **Accessible** — you can see all relevant options when choosing what to do
- **Unambiguous** — each item has a clear next action

Tools: physical inboxes, calendar, task manager, reference filing. The tool doesn't matter; the discipline does.

---

## Projects vs. Next Actions

A **project** is any desired outcome that requires more than one action step. GTD's definition is unusually broad — "tidy garage", "hire a designer", "write Q2 review" are all projects. 

Every project must have at least one **next action** — the very next visible, physical action that will move the project forward. Without a next action, projects stall.

This distinction is critical. People confuse project names ("Fix the bug") with actions. "Fix the bug" is a project. "Open the codebase and reproduce the crash" is a next action.

---

## Contexts

Next actions are organised by context — the tool, location, or mindset required:
- `@computer`, `@phone`, `@errands`, `@office`, `@home`, `@agenda-[person]`

Contexts let you batch similar actions. When you're near a phone, you scan `@phone` and knock out all calls at once. Modern GTD adapts contexts to energy level (`@high-energy`, `@low-focus`) or application (`@email`, `@code`).

---

## The Two-Minute Rule

If a clarified action takes less than two minutes, do it immediately — don't defer. The overhead of organising it exceeds the cost of just doing it. This is one of the most immediately impactful GTD habits.

---

## The Weekly Review

The most important GTD habit. Once a week, without exception:

1. **Get clear** — clear all inboxes, process all notes, get to inbox zero
2. **Get current** — review all Next Actions lists, review Projects, review Waiting For, review Someday/Maybe, review calendar past + future
3. **Get creative** — any new ideas? Any projects to add? Any wild cards?

The Weekly Review is what separates people who "tried GTD" from people who live by it. Without it, lists stagnate and trust in the system erodes.

---

## Natural Planning Model

How the brain naturally plans when working well. Five phases:

1. **Purpose / Principles** — Why are we doing this? What are our constraints?
2. **Outcome visioning** — What does success look like? (wild success, not hedged)
3. **Brainstorm** — Capture all ideas without judgment (classic brainstorm)
4. **Organise** — Identify components, sequences, priorities
5. **Identify next actions** — What moves this forward right now?

Most organisational planning skips directly to step 4 (organising) without doing the vision and brainstorm work. GTD argues this produces mediocre plans. The missing step is usually **outcome visioning**: many people can't describe what "done" looks like.

Contrast: the **Reactive Planning Model** — where the project has drifted into crisis and you're firefighting. Identifying purpose and vision at that stage feels indulgent, but it's what actually gets you out.

## Horizons of Focus

GTD acknowledges different altitudes of perspective, from ground-level actions up to life purpose:

| Horizon | Time frame | Examples |
|---------|-----------|---------|
| Ground: Next actions | Now | Send email, make call |
| Horizon 1: Projects | 1–2 years | Launch product, hire team |
| Horizon 2: Areas of focus | Ongoing | Health, relationships, finances |
| Horizon 3: Goals | 1–2 years | Career milestones, personal goals |
| Horizon 4: Vision | 3–5 years | Life direction |
| Horizon 5: Purpose | Lifetime | Why am I here? |

Most people neglect middle horizons (Areas of Focus, Goals). GTD doesn't prescribe a planning cadence for each level but ensures each horizon is reviewed periodically — areas during the Weekly Review, higher horizons in quarterly or annual reviews.

---

## The Someday/Maybe List

A parking lot for good ideas that aren't actionable now: "Learn Mandarin", "Start a podcast", "Visit Iceland". These are not trash — they're valid intentions. They live on Someday/Maybe and get reviewed weekly. This prevents the classic either/or: either commit to everything (overwhelming) or discard good ideas (loss).

---

## Reference vs. Actionable

A key clarifying distinction:
- **Reference material** — information you might need later; no action required. Goes in a filing system (physical or digital).
- **Actionable material** — requires a next action. Goes on Next Actions or Projects lists.

Most people's desks are a blurred mix of both, which creates anxiety (the brain scans everything as potentially requiring action). GTD separates them completely.

---

## Capture Triggers

Allen provides a comprehensive list of "incompletion triggers" — domains to sweep when capturing:
- Professional: projects started/not finished, commitments to boss/peers/reports, meetings to schedule, emails to write, systems to fix, training to do, etc.
- Personal: home repairs, family commitments, finances, health, travel, creative projects, reading list, etc.

The first capture sweep — getting everything out of your head — typically takes 1–6 hours and is described as "the most valuable thing you'll do in GTD."

## Key Distinctions

| Confused with | GTD clarifies |
|---------------|---------------|
| To-do lists | Context-organised Next Actions lists |
| Goals | Projects (concrete desired outcomes) |
| Inbox | Temporary capture point, not a to-do list |
| Calendar | Only hard landscape (time-specific, day-specific) |
| Urgency | Context + energy + time = actual choice |
