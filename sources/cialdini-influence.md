## Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion (1984, revised 2006, expanded 2021)

Cialdini's core thesis: **human beings use mental shortcuts (heuristics) to decide when to comply with requests** — and these shortcuts are predictable, universal, and exploitable. The book is both a scientific account of persuasion and a practical manual for ethical influence.

Cialdini spent three years in "training programs" for car salespeople, fund-raisers, advertising firms, and recruiters — observing influence in the wild before codifying the principles. The original six principles have been applied (and misapplied) more widely than almost any other framework in social psychology.

A seventh principle — **Unity** — was added in the 2021 expanded edition.

---

## The Seven Principles of Influence

### 1. Reciprocity

We feel obligated to return what others give us. This is one of the most deeply embedded social norms across every culture studied — the rule of reciprocity predates written society.

**Mechanism:** When someone gives us something (a gift, a favour, a concession), we experience psychological discomfort until we return it. This discomfort is exploited by:
- Free samples in supermarkets — the gift creates an obligation to buy
- The Hare Krishna movement's flower-and-then-ask model
- Negotiation: make a large ask, then concede to a smaller ask — the "concession" triggers reciprocal concession from the other side (the **door-in-the-face** technique)

**Key asymmetry:** The obligation can be triggered by uninvited gifts. You didn't ask for the flower, but now you feel obligated. The value of what is given back typically exceeds what was given. And the rule applies even when the initial gift was unwanted.

**Defence:** Recognise when a "gift" is actually a compliance tool. You can accept genuine gifts without incurring obligation; reframe the interaction.

---

### 2. Commitment and Consistency

Once we commit to a position, belief, or behaviour — especially publicly — we feel strong pressure to remain consistent with it.

**Mechanism:** We are motivated to appear consistent in our beliefs, words, and actions. Inconsistency is socially and psychologically uncomfortable. This is exploited by:
- The **foot-in-the-door** technique: get a small commitment first; larger requests follow more easily
- Written commitments: people who write down a commitment are far more likely to follow through
- Public commitments: stating something in front of others dramatically increases follow-through
- Car dealers: get you to write the offer — now you're committed

**The "lowball" technique:** Agree to a deal, then change the terms after the commitment is made. People often proceed anyway because they've already committed mentally.

**Why it's so powerful:** We use our past behaviour as evidence of our values. "I agreed to X, so I must value Y" — the commitment reshapes self-image.

**Defence:** Ask yourself: if I hadn't already committed, would I make this decision now? Separate the original commitment from the current decision.

---

### 3. Social Proof

When uncertain about the correct action, we look to what others are doing — especially others similar to us.

**Mechanism:** Social proof is a genuine information shortcut. In most situations, if many people are doing something, it probably is the right thing to do. The heuristic breaks down when:
- The crowd is wrong (pluralistic ignorance — everyone privately doubts but assumes others are certain)
- The evidence is manufactured (fake reviews, laugh tracks, inflated download counts)
- Emergency situations — diffusion of responsibility (the **bystander effect**: the more people present, the less likely any individual will help, because each assumes someone else will act)

**Applications:**
- "9 out of 10 dentists recommend..." — social proof from authority + peers
- Bartenders seeding tip jars
- Infomercials: "phones are ringing off the hook"
- Testimonials, reviews, follower counts

**The similarity effect:** Social proof is strongest from people *like us*. Peer behaviour is more influential than expert behaviour for many decisions.

**Defence:** Evaluate claims on their merits. Ask: is this crowd actually similar to me? Is the social proof genuine or manufactured?

### 4. Authority

We tend to comply with people who have legitimate authority or expertise — and with the *symbols* of authority, even absent genuine expertise.

**Mechanism:** Deference to authority is rational — experts usually do know more than we do. The shortcut fails when:
- Authority symbols are faked (titles, uniforms, credentials)
- True expertise in one domain is treated as authority in another
- The authority is manufactured or staged

**Classic examples:**
- Milgram experiments: ordinary people administered what they believed were dangerous electric shocks because a person in a lab coat told them to
- Doctors and nurses: nurses were found to comply with clearly incorrect medication orders from "doctors" (even over the phone) because of the authority symbol
- Titles, uniforms, expensive cars — all trigger automatic deference

**The contrast with genuine expertise:** Cialdini notes that genuine experts often signal their credibility by *acknowledging weaknesses* before presenting their case — this increases trust.

**Defence:** Ask: is this person actually an expert in this domain? Is the authority real or symbolic?

### 5. Liking

We are more easily persuaded by people we like. And we like people who are:
- **Similar** to us (background, interests, values, dress)
- **Familiar** (mere exposure effect — familiarity breeds liking, not contempt)
- **Attractive** (the halo effect — physical attractiveness transfers to perceived competence, honesty, intelligence)
- **Complimentary** — flattery works even when people know it's strategic
- **Associated with good things** — we like the bearer of good news; we dislike the bearer of bad (the messenger effect)

**Mechanism:** We use liking as a proxy for trust. If I like this person, they probably share my values, so I can trust their judgment. The shortcut is exploited by:
- Sales training: build rapport, find common ground, mirror the customer
- Celebrity endorsements — we like the celebrity; liking transfers to the product
- The Tupperware party model — you're buying from a friend, not a salesperson

**Association by proximity:** Merely being near good things (attractive people, prestigious brands) transfers some of their positive valence.

**Defence:** Separate liking the person from evaluating the offer. Ask: would I accept this if someone I didn't like made the same offer?

### 6. Scarcity

We value things more when they are rare, diminishing, or unavailable. Loss is more motivating than gain.

**Mechanism:** Two forces drive scarcity influence:
1. **Cognitive:** Rare things are usually more valuable — scarcity is a signal of quality
2. **Psychological reactance:** When our freedom to have something is threatened, we want it more (Brehm's reactance theory)

**Applications:**
- "Only 3 left in stock" (Amazon, Booking.com)
- Limited-time offers
- Exclusive access, invitation-only products
- The cookie experiment: identical cookies rated more desirable when in a jar with 2 than a jar with 10

**The competition intensifier:** Scarcity is most powerful when combined with competition. "Other buyers are looking at this right now" triggers both scarcity and social proof.

**Important nuance:** Things that *become* scarce (after being plentiful) are more desired than things that were always rare. The transition matters. This explains the intensity of censorship backlash — restricting something previously available is more provocative than never having allowed it.

**Defence:** Ask: do I actually want this, or am I responding to the threatened loss? Separate the value of the thing from the scarcity signal.

---

### 7. Unity (added 2021)

The newest principle. We are strongly influenced by people we perceive as *us* — members of our in-group, our tribe, our family. This goes beyond liking (which is about the individual) — Unity is about **shared identity**.

**Mechanism:** We act differently with in-group members. We extend trust, forgiveness, effort, and generosity that we don't extend to out-group members — even when the in-group member is a stranger.

**Sources of Unity:**
- Family and kinship (most powerful)
- Shared place — where you're from, your neighbourhood, your alma mater
- Shared experience — having gone through the same thing
- Co-creation — being involved in making something together; asking for advice (transforms the relationship from target to collaborator)

**The "ask for advice" technique:** People who are asked for their advice — not their opinion, their *advice* — feel a sense of ownership in the outcome. They become invested in the success of the person they advised.

**Defence:** Recognise when a sense of "we" is being manufactured. Evaluate the offer itself, not your group membership.

## The Unified Framework: Automatic Compliance

All seven principles share a common structure:

**Trigger → Automatic response → Compliance**

The trigger (a gift, a commitment, a crowd, an authority, a familiar face, a scarce item, a shared identity) activates a cognitive shortcut that was learned because it was *usually* correct. The shortcut saves mental energy and usually produces good outcomes. It becomes exploitable only when the trigger is present without the underlying validity.

Cialdini calls these **click-whirr** responses — like a cassette tape: once the right trigger is pressed, the behaviour plays out automatically.

---

## Ethical Use vs. Manipulation

Cialdini draws a clear line:
- **Ethical influence:** Use the principles to help people see genuine value that they might otherwise miss due to their own heuristics
- **Manipulation:** Trigger automatic compliance for something that is not genuinely in the target's interest

The distinction matters practically as well as ethically: manipulation builds short-term compliance but destroys trust and long-term relationship value. Cialdini's research on negotiation shows that ethical use of reciprocity, commitment, and social proof produces more durable outcomes than manipulation.
