## Book 1: Competitive Strategy (1980)

### Core Thesis

Industry structure — the configuration of competitive forces within an industry — is the primary determinant of the average profitability of firms in that industry. A firm that understands its industry structure can position itself to earn above-average returns. Strategy is about choosing a position within industry structure that is defensible.

Porter broke from the traditional view that strategy is about setting goals and marshalling resources. His argument was fundamentally **economic**: structure → conduct → performance. The structure of an industry shapes the conduct of its firms, which in turn determines collective performance. Firms that understand this structural reality can position advantageously within it.

### Key Frameworks Introduced

#### 1. The Five Forces Framework

Industry profitability is determined by five competitive forces:

1. **Threat of New Entrants** — how easily can new competitors enter? Barriers to entry determine the answer: economies of scale, brand identity, switching costs, capital requirements, access to distribution, proprietary technology, government policy.

2. **Bargaining Power of Suppliers** — how much power do input suppliers have? High when: suppliers are concentrated, input is critical, switching costs are high, supplier can forward-integrate.

3. **Bargaining Power of Buyers** — how much power do customers have? High when: buyers are concentrated, volume is large, product is undifferentiated, buyer can backward-integrate, buyer is price sensitive.

4. **Threat of Substitute Products or Services** — how easily can customers switch to something different that performs the same function? Substitutes cap industry prices.

5. **Rivalry Among Existing Competitors** — how intense is competition within the industry? Determined by number of competitors, industry growth rate, fixed costs, differentiation, switching costs, exit barriers.

The collective strength of these forces determines **industry attractiveness** (average long-run profit potential). The goal of competitive strategy is to find a position in the industry where the company can best defend itself against these forces, or can influence them in its favour.

#### 2. Three Generic Strategies

Three internally consistent approaches for outperforming industry competitors:

1. **Overall Cost Leadership** — achieve the lowest delivered cost in the industry through scale, efficiency, cost control. Requires: heavy capital investment, aggressive pricing, tight control over overhead, experience curve exploitation.

2. **Differentiation** — create something perceived industry-wide as unique (product features, brand image, technology, customer service, dealer network). Customers pay premium. Requires: strong marketing, product engineering, creative flair, strong basic research, long history in the industry.

3. **Focus** — serve a particular buyer group, product segment, or geographic market. A low-cost focus or a differentiation focus within a narrow target.

**"Stuck in the Middle"**: Firms that fail to pursue one of these strategies clearly end up in a low-profit position — they fail to win on either cost or differentiation. This is Porter's most quoted warning.

#### 3. Industry Structural Analysis

Porter provides a framework for systematically analysing industry structure. The five forces analysis is the tool; structural analysis is the process of applying it to understand:
- Where does bargaining power sit?
- What are the barriers to entry?
- What signals will structural change?
- Which force is dominant in this industry?

#### 4. Competitor Analysis

Framework for predicting competitor behaviour: future goals, current strategy, assumptions about the industry, capabilities. The goal is to anticipate competitive response to your moves.

#### 5. Competitive Moves

First-mover vs follower analysis, cooperative vs. competitive moves, commitment and signalling theory.

#### 6. Industry Evolution

Industries are not static. Porter analyses how industries evolve through their life cycle — and what changes in competitive structure this produces: commoditisation, shakeout, maturation, internationalisation. Different generic strategies become appropriate at different stages.

## Book 2: Competitive Advantage (1985)

### Core Thesis

Competitive advantage grows fundamentally from the value a firm creates for its buyers. Value creation must exceed the costs of creating it. There are two basic types of competitive advantage: **cost leadership** (doing the same things as competitors but at lower cost) and **differentiation** (doing things differently so that buyers are willing to pay a premium). Both can be achieved broadly or narrowly (focus). The value chain is the tool for identifying and analysing sources of competitive advantage.

### Key Frameworks Introduced

#### 1. The Value Chain

The value chain disaggregates the firm into its strategically relevant activities — the discrete steps through which a firm creates, delivers, and supports its product. Competitive advantage comes from performing these activities more cheaply or in ways that create greater buyer value than competitors.

**Primary Activities:**
- Inbound Logistics — receiving, warehousing, inventory control of inputs
- Operations — transforming inputs into final product
- Outbound Logistics — distributing finished product
- Marketing & Sales — advertising, channel management, pricing
- Service — post-sale activities (installation, maintenance, warranty)

**Support Activities:**
- Firm Infrastructure — general management, planning, finance, legal
- Human Resource Management — recruiting, training, compensation
- Technology Development — R&D, process improvement, automation
- Procurement — purchasing of inputs used throughout the value chain

**Margin** = Total value created − Total cost of performing activities

**Linkages** within and between value chains: The value chain of a firm is embedded in a larger value system that includes supplier value chains and buyer value chains. Competitive advantage can come from optimising linkages across this broader system, not just within the firm.

#### 2. Cost Drivers and Value Drivers

Porter systematically analyses what causes cost in each activity (scale, learning, capacity utilisation, interrelationships, integration, timing, location, policy choices) and what determines buyer value/willingness to pay.

#### 3. Competitive Scope

Scope of competition affects competitive advantage through: segment scope (which buyer segments to serve), vertical scope (make vs buy), geographic scope (national vs global), industry scope (which related industries to compete in).

#### 4. Technology and Competitive Advantage

Technology permeates the value chain. Technological change can alter relative competitive position — both threatening existing advantage and creating new opportunities. Porter analyses how firms should think about R&D priorities through the value chain lens.

#### 5. Competitor Selection

Not all competitors are enemies. Some competitors can strengthen a firm's competitive position by increasing buyer demand, improving industry structure, deterring entry, and providing benchmarks for comparison.

## Key Quotes

> "The essence of strategy formulation is coping with competition." (*Competitive Strategy*, 1980)

> "The five competitive forces reveal that competition in an industry goes well beyond the established players. Customers, suppliers, substitutes, and potential entrants are all 'competitors' to firms in the industry and may be more or less prominent depending on particular circumstances." (*Competitive Strategy*, 1980)

> "Competitive advantage cannot be understood by looking at a firm as a whole. It stems from the many discrete activities a firm performs in designing, producing, marketing, delivering, and supporting its product." (*Competitive Advantage*, 1985)

> "Competitive advantage grows out of value a firm is able to create for its buyers that exceeds the firm's cost of creating it." (*Competitive Advantage*, 1985)

> "The best strategy is to be the best at what you do for the customers you serve." — paraphrase of Porter's positioning logic

> "If you don't have a competitive advantage, don't compete." — Porter

> "Strategy is the creation of a unique and valuable position, involving a different set of activities." (*What Is Strategy?*, HBR 1996)

> "The company without a strategy is willing to try anything." — Porter

## Why It Matters / Legacy

Porter's two books transformed strategic management into a rigorous analytical discipline. Before Porter, strategy was largely descriptive and anecdotal — war metaphors and CEO intuition. Porter imported industrial organisation economics (IO economics) into management, giving executives the analytical tools to reason systematically about competitive position.

**Legacy:**
- Five Forces is used in virtually every business school strategy curriculum worldwide
- The value chain is the standard tool for operations and cost analysis
- Generic strategies are the starting vocabulary of competitive positioning
- Porter's framework was the dominant paradigm from 1980–mid-1990s

**The challenge from Resource-Based View (RBV):**
Jay Barney, Birger Wernerfelt, and C.K. Prahalad/Gary Hamel challenged Porter's outside-in view in the late 1980s/early 1990s. Where Porter says "analyse your industry, then position", RBV says "analyse your internal resources and capabilities; they are the primary source of sustainable competitive advantage." Prahalad's core competency framework is the most widely known RBV challenge.

## About Porter

Michael E. Porter (born 1947). Bishop William Lawrence University Professor at Harvard Business School, the highest honour the university bestows. His 1980 *Competitive Strategy* and 1985 *Competitive Advantage* are among the most influential business books ever written. His 1996 HBR article *What Is Strategy?* is considered essential reading. Additional works include *The Competitive Advantage of Nations* (1990) and work on clusters, healthcare, and social responsibility (shared value). He is the most-cited scholar in economics and business combined.
