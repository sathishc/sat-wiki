# "What the Bubble Got Right" — Paul Graham (2004)

## Bibliographic Info

- **Author:** Paul Graham
- **Published:** September 2004
- **Origin:** Talk at ICFP 2004, expanded into essay

## Central Argument

The dot-com bubble was financially irrational but directionally correct. The ideas were right; the valuations and timelines were wrong. Businesses built on the Internet, programmers being the key resource, remote work, and open source software — all of these were vindicated. The bubble's error was a timing and valuation mistake, not a strategic mistake. The lesson: when the market gets something badly wrong in magnitude, check whether it got the direction right.

## Key Ideas

1. **The web changed everything — just not as fast as people thought.** The 1999 belief that the Internet would transform commerce and communication was correct. The 1999 timeline was laughably wrong. The lesson: discount timing claims but not directional ones.
2. **Programmers are the key resource.** The bubble was right that the scarcest, most valuable input to tech companies is talent, not capital. The competition for programmers was not irrational — it was just priced wrong.
3. **Remote work works.** Companies during the bubble started experimenting with distributed teams. The idea was right; the infrastructure wasn't ready yet.
4. **Open source was underrated.** The open source movement was dismissed during the bubble as naive. It turned out to be one of the most important structural shifts in software development.
5. **Consumer preference for free.** The bubble companies were right that people would strongly prefer free services and would give up a lot of privacy and attention to get them. This turned out to be correct.
6. **The "price discovery" function of bubbles.** Bubbles force exploration of ideas that wouldn't get funded in calmer times. They overprice the ideas, yes, but the ideas get tried. Some of the tries work.

## Notable Quotes

> "I had a front row seat for the Internet Bubble, because I worked at Yahoo during 1998 and 1999. One day, when the stock was trading around $200, I sat down and calculated what I thought the price should be. The answer I got was $12."

> "The Internet did transform commerce and communication, just not in the specific way or on the specific timeline that investors expected in 1999. The error was mostly in timing and valuation, not in direction."

> "What the bubble got right: the web matters, programmers matter, remote work is viable, open source is powerful, consumers want free. All correct. Just priced wrong and timed wrong."
