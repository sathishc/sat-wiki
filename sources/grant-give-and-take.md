# Give and Take

**Author:** Adam Grant  
**Published:** 2013  
**Publisher:** Viking / Penguin  
**Type:** Non-fiction, organisational psychology

---

## Summary

Based on Grant's research at Wharton, *Give and Take* argues that the most important — and most underrated — dimension of professional success is **reciprocity style**: how you approach giving and taking in your relationships and work.

Grant identifies three styles:

| Style | Behaviour |
|-------|-----------|
| **Taker** | Takes more than gives; self-promotional; zero-sum mindset |
| **Matcher** | Maintains balance; returns favours in kind; tit-for-tat |
| **Giver** | Contributes without keeping score; other-focused |

The central, counterintuitive finding: **givers are at both the bottom and the top of success**. Selfless givers — those who give without any regard for their own needs — get exploited and burn out. They populate the bottom of success ladders.

*Otherish givers* — those who are genuinely generous but also set limits, attend to their own interests, and give in ways that are sustainable — consistently outperform takers and matchers over time. They populate the top.

---

## Key Concepts

### Givers, Takers, and Matchers
The three reciprocity styles are not fixed character types — they are *strategies* people adopt, sometimes varying by context. The most successful people are otherish givers: motivated primarily by others' interests, but not to the exclusion of their own.

### The Five-Minute Favour
Grant's heuristic for sustainable giving: give in ways that take less than five minutes for you but can be enormously valuable to someone else. Introductions, feedback, sharing information. High value-to-cost ratio.

### Dormant Ties
Givers are natural network builders because they help without expecting immediate return. This creates a wide, diverse network of people who remember the giver positively. Dormant ties — connections that have gone quiet — are often the highest-value sources of novel information and opportunity (because they've moved in different circles since you last spoke).

### Perspective-Taking and Creativity
Givers are more naturally inclined to take others' perspectives — they spend more time thinking about others' needs. Paradoxically, this makes them *more* creative: perspective-taking expands the space of possibilities by allowing the giver to see problems from multiple angles.

### Fragile Organisations Full of Takers
When an organisation is dominated by takers, several pathologies emerge:
- Information is hoarded (sharing means giving away an advantage)
- Collaboration collapses (every interaction is zero-sum)
- Trust is absent (everyone is suspected of self-serving motives)
- The stars are self-promotional, not necessarily the most productive

The result: the organisation looks impressive on paper but underperforms, especially in collaborative or complex work.

### The Giver's Dilemma
Givers face a genuine strategic challenge: their generosity can be exploited by takers, particularly early in a relationship before the other party's style is known. Grant's prescription: screen for reciprocity style; be more cautious with clear takers; match with matchers; invest heavily in other givers.
